Day 1 
Mills criticized older sociology because it collected too many facts only for administration and decision-making, instead of asking meaningful social questions. In simple words, Mills wanted sociology to be more thoughtful and socially important, not just a collection of statistics. 
He also said true social science must focus on substantive problems. That means it should deal with real issues that matter in human life and connect them to historical and structural conditions. 
Then there is a line from Mills about reason in democracy. The meaning is that sociology should help people think clearly and use reason in public life. It should support a free society by helping citizens understand what is happening around them 
Mills’ Eight Principles of Intellectual Craft 
These are like guidelines for how a sociologist or serious thinker should work. 
The first principle is integrate work and life. This means do not separate your thinking from your everyday experiences. Your life can help your work, and your work can deepen your life. Sociology is not something done only during office hours; it is a way of observing and understanding the world all the time. 
The second is keep a file. This means maintain notes, ideas, observations, and 
experiences in an organized way. It helps preserve thoughts and improves writing 
discipline. In modern terms, this can be a notebook, journal, or digital document where you collect ideas. 
The third is continual review. This means you should regularly reflect on your ideas and experiences. Do not think only when an exam or deadline comes. Keep reviewing and refining your understanding. 
The fourth is learn from bad books. This is actually a very smart point. Even a bad book can teach you something. It can show weak arguments, poor logic, or limited thinking, which helps sharpen your own understanding. So reading should always be active, not passive. 
The fifth is playfulness with ideas. This means you should explore words, thoughts, and concepts creatively. Sociology needs seriousness, but also curiosity and imagination. You should not be afraid to test ideas and look at things in new ways. 


The sixth is perspective of others. To understand society well, you should try to see the world from other viewpoints — not only from your own. Historians, biologists, workers, industrialists, and ordinary people may all see the same world differently. This broadens your imagination. 
The seventh is imaginative extremes. In the early stage of thinking, Mills says do not be afraid to think in bold or extreme ways. This does not mean being unrealistic forever, but it means allowing your mind to explore possibilities before narrowing down the best answer. 
The eighth is simple, direct language. This is very important. Mills believed ideas should be expressed clearly and simply. If language is too heavy, confusing, or dead, then imagination also becomes weak. Good sociology should be understandable. 
So the whole lesson here is teaching you two big things. First, societies have boundaries, membership, and different forms, including online forms. Second, sociology is special because it helps us connect our personal life with wider social forces. That deeper way of thinking is called the sociological imagination. 
Socialization 
Socialization means the process of learning how to live in society. 
When a baby is born, the baby has biological needs like hunger, crying, sleeping, and fear. But the baby does not know language, manners, rules, values, religion, culture, gender roles, school behavior, or how to communicate properly. Through family, school, friends, media, religion, and society, the child slowly learns how to behave like a member of society. This learning process is called socialization. 
In simple words: 
Socialization is the process by which humans learn culture and become proper members of society.
For example, you learned how to speak Sinhala/Tamil/English, how to greet elders, how to behave in school, how to dress for different places, how to respect teachers, and how to understand right and wrong. You were not born knowing all these things. You learned them through socialization. 
Socialization is a lifelong process 
The slide says socialization is a lifelong process. This means it does not stop after childhood. 


Many people think socialization only happens when we are small children. But actually, we continue learning new social roles throughout life. 
For example, when you first go to university, you learn how to behave as an undergraduate. When you start a job, you learn workplace culture, professional communication, deadlines, meetings, and teamwork. When someone becomes a parent, they learn how to act as a mother or father. When people grow old, they also learn new roles in family and society. So socialization continues from birth to death. 
Human potential 
The slide says socialization helps people develop human potential. 
This means socialization helps humans develop abilities such as language, thinking, emotions, relationships, and complex communication. 
A human baby has the ability to learn language, but language will not develop properly without social interaction. A child must hear others speaking, respond to others, and be corrected by others. In the same way, emotions such as love, shame, respect, sympathy, and guilt are also developed through social life. 
So society helps humans become fully human. Without socialization, a person may not develop normal human behavior, communication, or emotional understanding. 
Culture transmission 
Another important function of socialization is culture transmission. 
Culture includes values, norms, beliefs, customs, traditions, language, religion, and practices. Socialization passes these from one generation to the next. 
For example, parents teach children how to celebrate New Year, how to respect elders, what food habits are common, what language to speak, what religious practices to follow, and what behavior is considered polite or rude. 
This is how culture continues. Without socialization, each generation would have to start from zero. Society would not be stable. 
Personality formation 
The slide also says socialization helps in personality formation. 
Personality means the unique pattern of thinking, feeling, and behaving that makes a person different from others. 


For example, one person may be friendly and talkative, another may be quiet and serious, another may be creative and emotional. Sociology asks: how much of this personality comes from society? 
The slide says personality is substantially acquired through socialization, but there is a debate. That debate is called Nature vs Nurture. 
Personality: Nature vs Nurture 
This is the biggest idea in this slide. 
Nature means biological factors. 
Nurture means social and environmental factors. 
The question is: 
Are we born with our personality, or do we learn it from society?
Sociology usually gives more attention to nurture, but it does not completely ignore nature. 
Nature – Biological view 
The biological view says some parts of human behavior come from biology, genetics, and evolution. 
The slide mentions Darwin. Darwin’s theory explains that humans share an evolutionary background with other species. This means some human behaviors may come from survival needs. 
For example, fear can protect us from danger. Hunger pushes us to find food. Reproduction helps the continuation of the species. These are not learned from society first; they are biological drives. 
The slide also mentions instinct. 
Instinct means unlearned behavior that exists naturally. For example, a baby crying when hungry, fear of danger, or the desire to survive can be seen as instinctive behaviors. Then it mentions heredity. 
Heredity means genetic inheritance from parents. Some people may be naturally more calm, active, sensitive, intelligent, or emotionally reactive because of genetic factors. So biology can influence temperament and certain abilities. 
But the slide asks an important question: can we say someone is “naturally aggressive” or “naturally creative”? This is difficult, because society also affects those behaviors. A 


person may have a biological tendency, but the environment can increase, reduce, or shape it. 
For example, a child may be naturally energetic, but whether that child becomes confident, aggressive, disciplined, or creative depends a lot on family, school, and society. 
Nurture – Sociological view 
The sociological view focuses on nurture. 
Nurture means environment, learning, culture, family, education, friends, media, and social experiences. 
The slide mentions John B. Watson, a behaviorist. He argued that personality and behavior are mainly shaped by the environment and conditioning, not genetics. 
Conditioning means learning through rewards, punishments, and repeated experiences. 
For example, if a child is praised for speaking politely, the child learns to continue that behavior. If a child is punished for shouting, the child may reduce that behavior. If a child grows up in a loving and supportive home, they may develop confidence. If a child grows up in a violent environment, they may develop fear, anger, or defensive behavior. 
So according to nurture, people become who they are because of what they learn from society. 
Freud’s idea: Eros and Thanatos 
The slide also mentions two instincts: Eros and Thanatos. 
These ideas come from Sigmund Freud. 
Eros means the life instinct. It is connected with love, survival, care, creativity, and positive connection. 
Thanatos means the death or aggression instinct. It is connected with aggression, destruction, anger, and harmful impulses. 
Freud believed human personality is shaped by the tension between these instincts. But sociology would say that society teaches us how to control these instincts. 
For example, a person may feel anger, but society teaches that we cannot simply attack others. We must control anger, communicate, follow laws, and behave acceptably. This control happens through socialization. 
Can animals, AI, and robots be socialized? 


The last part of the slide raises an interesting question. 
It asks whether animals, AI, and robots can be socialized and whether they can have personality. 
Animals can be trained and can learn behavior from humans or other animals. For example, dogs learn commands, routines, and acceptable behavior. 
AI and robots can also be programmed or trained to respond in certain ways. They may appear to have personality, like being friendly, formal, funny, or helpful. But the question is whether this is real personality or only programmed behavior. 
In sociology, true socialization usually means learning culture through social experience. 
So humans are the main focus, but modern technology makes this question more interesting. 
Sociology as a Science 
When we hear the word science, we usually think about subjects like Biology, Chemistry, or Physics. But sociology is also a science because it studies society in a systematic, organized, and evidence-based way. 
Sociology does not simply guess about society. It observes social life, collects data, analyzes patterns, and gives explanations based on evidence. That is why sociology is called a social science. 
Natural sciences study the physical or natural world. For example, Biology studies living organisms, and Physics studies matter and energy. But social sciences study human beings, society, and social behavior. Sociology belongs to this category. 
Sociology – Definition 
The slide defines sociology as: 
“The systematic study of human society and social interaction.”Let’s break this definition simply. 
Systematic study means sociology follows an organized method. It does not depend only on personal opinion. Sociologists collect information, compare facts, identify patterns, and explain why things happen in society. 
Human society means groups of people living together with shared rules, culture, institutions, and relationships. 


Social interaction means the way people communicate, behave, influence each other, and form relationships. For example, family relationships, teacher-student relationships, workplace communication, online interactions, and religious/community activities are all social interactions. 
So, in simple words: 
Sociology studies how people live together, how they behave in groups, and how society influences their lives.
Society 
The slide again reminds us that society is a large social group that shares territory, political authority, and cultural expectations. 
For example, Sri Lankan society has a geographical territory, a government, laws, cultural values, traditions, languages, religions, and social expectations. These things help organize people’s lives. 
Sociology 
Sociology is the academic subject that studies society, its structures, processes, and changes. 
Here, structures mean organized parts of society, such as family, education, religion, economy, law, and government. 
Processes mean ongoing social activities such as socialization, communication, conflict, cooperation, migration, urbanization, and social change. 
Changes mean how society transforms over time. For example, technology has changed education, relationships, jobs, business, and communication. Sociology studies these changes. 
Sociologist 
A sociologist is a person who studies society using sociological methods. 
A sociologist may study topics such as poverty, unemployment, gender inequality, crime, education, family life, social media behavior, religion, politics, or youth culture. 
For example, instead of saying “young people are addicted to social media,” a sociologist may ask: Why do young people use social media so much? What social needs does it fulfill? How does it affect identity, friendship, education, and mental well-being? 
So sociologists try to understand the deeper social reasons behind human behavior. 


Levels of Analysis 
The slide mentions three levels of analysis: micro, meso, and macro. 
Micro level means small-scale social interaction. It focuses on individuals and face-to-face interactions. For example, how two friends communicate, how a teacher interacts with students, or how family members behave at home. 
Meso level means middle-level social organization. It focuses on groups, organizations, and communities. For example, schools, universities, workplaces, religious groups, and online communities. 
Macro level means large-scale society-wide structures. It focuses on big institutions and systems such as government, economy, education system, religion, social class, and culture. 
A simple example is unemployment. 
At the micro level, we can study how unemployment affects one person’s confidence and family life. 
At the meso level, we can study how a company or university prepares people for jobs. 
At the macro level, we can study how the national economy, government policy, and labour market create unemployment. 
So sociology can study the same issue at different levels. 
Sociological Perspective 
The sociological perspective means looking at individual lives through the larger patterns of society. 
It helps us understand that people’s lives are shaped by social forces such as family background, education, culture, economy, gender, class, religion, and politics. 
For example, if one student fails an exam, we may first think it is only that student’s problem. But sociology asks bigger questions: Did the student have access to good education? Did family problems affect learning? Was there financial stress? Was the education system fair? 
So sociological perspective helps us look beyond the surface. 
Sociological Imagination 
The slide again mentions sociological imagination, introduced by C. Wright Mills. 


Sociological imagination means the ability to connect personal troubles with public issues. 
For example, if one person cannot find a job, it may be a personal trouble. But if many graduates cannot find jobs, it becomes a public issue. It may be connected to the economy, education system, government policy, or lack of industry opportunities. So sociological imagination teaches us that personal problems are often connected to wider society. 
Sociology and the Scientific Method 
Now the slide explains the scientific method. 
Science is defined as a body of systematically arranged knowledge that explains general laws or patterns. Since sociology also follows systematic research methods, it is considered a science. 
The scientific method has several steps. 
1. Review of Literature 
The first step is review of literature. 
This means the sociologist studies existing research about the topic. They read books, journal articles, reports, and previous studies to understand what is already known. 
For example, if a sociologist wants to study social media addiction among university students, they first read previous research about social media use, student behavior, mental health, and online culture. 
The purpose is to identify what researchers already know and what gaps still exist. In your research project also, this is like doing a literature review before proposing your own study. 
2. Formulate Hypotheses 
The second step is to formulate hypotheses. 
A hypothesis is a testable statement or prediction. 
For example: 
“University students who spend more than five hours per day on social media are more likely to experience academic distraction.”


This is a hypothesis because it predicts a relationship between two variables: social media usage and academic distraction. 
A good hypothesis must be testable using data. 
3. Choose Research Design 
The third step is to choose a research design. 
This means selecting the best method to study the problem. 
Common sociological research methods include surveys, experiments, ethnography, interviews, observation, and content analysis. 
For example, if the researcher wants to collect information from many students, they may use a survey. If they want deep personal opinions, they may use interviews. If they want to study online comments, they may use content analysis. If they want to observe a 
community closely, they may use ethnography. 
So the research design depends on the research question. 
4. Gather Data 
The fourth step is to gather data. 
This means collecting information in an organized way. 
Data can be collected through questionnaires, interviews, direct observation, documents, social media posts, government reports, school records, or experiments. 
For example, in a study about university students, data may be collected using Google Forms, interviews, or academic records. 
The important thing is that data should be collected carefully and ethically. 
5. Analyse Data 
The fifth step is to analyse data. 
After collecting data, the sociologist examines it to identify patterns, relationships, and meanings. 
There are two main types of analysis: 
Quantitative analysis uses numbers and statistics. For example, percentages, charts, averages, and correlations. 


Qualitative analysis uses words, meanings, and interpretations. For example, analyzing interview answers, stories, opinions, or social media comments. 
For example, if 70% of students say social media affects their study time, that is a quantitative finding. If students explain that they use social media because of stress or loneliness, that is qualitative information. 
6. Draw Conclusions 
The final step is to draw conclusions. 
This means the researcher explains what the findings mean. They check whether the hypothesis was supported or not. 
For example, if the data shows that heavy social media users have more academic distraction, the hypothesis may be supported. If the data does not show that relationship, the hypothesis may be rejected or revised. 
After that, researchers may publish their findings so others can read, criticize, repeat, or improve the study. 
This is important because science grows through checking and further research. 

about a famous moral philosophy thought experiment called The Runaway Trolley. It is not exactly sociology, but it connects to ethics, justice, moral reasoning, and how people make decisions. 


The main question is: 
Is it right to sacrifice one person to save many people?
This lesson is trying to show that moral decisions are not always simple. Sometimes two choices both feel wrong, but we still have to decide what is more acceptable. 
1. The Runaway Trolley — Main Idea 
A trolley means a small train/tram. In this thought experiment, the trolley is moving and the brakes have failed. It is going to hit and kill five workers on the track. 
You have a choice: you can turn the trolley onto another track where there is one worker. If you turn it, the five workers will be saved, but the one worker will die. 
So the question is: 
Should you turn the trolley and kill one person to save five people?
Most people say yes, because they think saving five lives is better than saving one. Even though one person dies, the result is fewer deaths. 
This is called a moral dilemma, because there is no perfect answer. Whatever you do, someone may die. 
2. Scenario 1 — The Trolley Driver 
In the first scenario, you are the driver. The trolley’s brakes have failed. On the main track, five workers will die. But you can turn the trolley to a side track, where one worker will die. 
Most people say: 
“Turn the trolley.”
Why? Because they think it is better to save five people, even if one person dies. This is based on the idea of greater good. 
In simple words: 
Killing one is tragic, but allowing five to die feels worse.
Here, the death of one person is not your main goal. Your goal is to save five people. The one person’s death is a side effect of turning the trolley. 
That is why many people feel this action is morally acceptable. 
3. Scenario 2 — The Bridge Bystander 


The second scenario changes the situation. 
Now you are standing on a bridge above the track. Again, the trolley is going to kill five workers. There is a heavy man standing near you. If you push him onto the track, his body will stop the trolley. He will die, but the five workers will be saved. 
The result is exactly the same as the first case: One person dies, five people are saved.
But most people say: 
“No, I will not push him.”
Why? Because pushing someone with your own hands feels much more wrong. Here, you are directly using the person as a tool to stop the trolley. 
So even though the numbers are the same, people feel differently. 
That is the puzzle. 
4. The Moral Puzzle 
The slide compares the two scenarios. 
In both cases, the numbers are the same: 
5 lives vs 1 life.
But our moral reaction changes. 
In the driver case, most people say turning the trolley is right. 
In the bridge case, most people say pushing the heavy man is wrong. 
This shows that moral reasoning is not only about numbers. It is also about how the action is done. 
The slide compares these differences: 
In Scenario 1, your role is driver. You are already involved in the situation. In Scenario 2, your role is bystander. You are not already part of the accident. 
In Scenario 1, the action is turning a steering wheel. In Scenario 2, the action is pushing a person with your hands. 
In both cases, the victim did not consent. That means the one person did not agree to die. 


But in Scenario 1, the death is seen as a side effect. In Scenario 2, the person’s death becomes a means to an end. That means you are using the person’s death as the method to save others. 
This is why people judge the two cases differently. 
5. Three Possible Explanations 
The slides give three explanations for why turning feels acceptable but pushing feels wrong. The first explanation is using a person as a means. 
In the bridge case, the heavy man is used like a tool. His body becomes the method for stopping the trolley. This feels morally wrong because humans should not be treated like objects. 
In the driver case, the one worker on the side track is not being used as a tool. He is an unfortunate victim of the decision to redirect the trolley. So some people think the driver case is less wrong. 
The second explanation is the role of intention. 
In the driver case, your intention is to save five people. You do not directly want the one worker to die. His death is foreseen, but it is not the main purpose. 
In the bridge case, the heavy man must die in order to stop the trolley. His death is part of the plan. So the intention feels more morally problematic. 
The third explanation is physical directness of action. 
Pushing someone with your hands feels more emotionally and morally disturbing than turning a wheel. Turning a wheel feels indirect. Pushing someone feels direct and personal. 
But the slide also asks an important question: is this a real moral difference, or just a psychological feeling? 
That means maybe pushing feels worse only because it is more physically uncomfortable, not because it is morally different. 
6. The Trap-Door Twist 
This slide introduces a new version of the problem. 
Imagine you are still on the bridge, but this time you do not push the heavy man. Instead, you turn a wheel that opens a trap-door under him. He falls onto the track, dies, and saves the five workers. 


Now there is no physical pushing. 
So the question becomes: 
If pushing is wrong because of physical contact, is opening the trap-door acceptable? This twist tests our moral thinking. 
If the problem is only “hands-on pushing,” then opening the trap-door should feel okay, because you are just turning a wheel. 
But many people still feel it is wrong. 
Why? 
Because the real issue may not be physical contact. The real issue is that you are deliberately causing one person’s death and using him as a means to save others. So this twist shows that the deeper moral problem is not simply touching or pushing. It is about intention and using a human being as a tool. 
7. What the Trolley Teaches Us 
The trolley problem teaches us three important lessons. 
First, it shows conflicting moral principles. 
One principle says: save as many lives as possible. 
Another principle says: never kill an innocent person, even for a good reason. 
Both principles sound reasonable. But in the trolley problem, they clash. That is why the decision is difficult. 
Second, it shows the value of hypotheticals. 
A hypothetical situation means an imaginary situation used to test ideas. The trolley problem may not happen in real life exactly like this, but it helps us think clearly about moral principles. 
It removes extra confusion and asks us to focus on the core question: what makes an action right or wrong? 
Third, it shows moral reasoning as self-discovery. 
This means the trolley problem helps us understand our own moral beliefs. When we answer these questions, we learn what matters to us: consequences, intention, human dignity, responsibility, or emotional reaction. 


So moral reasoning is not only about convincing others. It is also about understanding ourselves. 
8. For Discussion Questions 
The final slide gives discussion questions. Here is how you can understand them. 
Question 1: Is it ever morally right to kill one person to save five? 
This asks whether saving more lives can justify sacrificing one innocent person. 
Question 2: Does the method matter — wheel vs hands? 
This asks whether turning a wheel is morally different from physically pushing someone. 
Question 3: Are the driver and bystander equally responsible? 
This asks whether your role matters. The driver is already part of the accident, but the bystander becomes involved by choosing to act. 
Question 4: Does intention distinguish the two cases? 
This asks whether there is a difference between foreseeing a death and intending a death. 
Question 5: What does the trap-door reveal? 
This asks whether the real moral issue is physical contact, or something deeper, like using a person as a means. 
Day 2 Social Structure 
This part mainly explains Status. 
Social Structure 
In sociology, social structure means the organized arrangement of people, groups, institutions, statuses, roles, norms, and values in society. 
Think of society like a big system. In that system, every person has a place, every place has expected duties, and every duty is guided by rules and values. For example, in a family, there are parents, children, grandparents, brothers, and sisters. Each person has a position and expected behavior. That is a small example of social structure. 
The four main words in the slide are: 


Status means a person’s position in society. 
Role means the behavior expected from that position. 
Norms mean the rules of behavior. 
Values mean the beliefs about what is important or good. 
This slide focuses on the first one: Status. 
Meaning of Status 
In general meaning, status means the position a person occupies in a group or society. For example, in a family, someone can have the status of mother, father, child, sister, or brother. In a university, someone can have the status of student, lecturer, dean, or administrator. 
But in sociology, status means more than just position. It also includes the person’s social standing, prestige, respect, and honour in society. 
For example, both a doctor and a shop assistant have positions in society. But society may give more prestige or respect to the status of doctor. That higher respect is part of social status. 
The slide says status refers to what a person is, while role refers to what a person does. This is very important. 
For example: 
A person is a teacher. That is status. 
A teacher teaches students, prepares lessons, marks papers, and guides students. That is role. 
A person is a parent. That is status. 
A parent takes care of children, protects them, teaches them, and supports them. That is role. 
So the easiest way to remember: 
Status = position 
Role = expected behavior of that position 
R. Linton defined status as a position in a social system, such as child or parent. That means status tells us where a person is located in the social system. 
Definitions of Status 


The slide gives definitions from several sociologists. You do not need to memorize every word, but you should understand the meaning. 
Kingsley Davis says status is a position in the general institutional system, recognized by the whole society. It is not always deliberately created. It develops naturally through customs, traditions, and folkways. 
In simple words, society gives certain positions to people, and people recognize those positions through culture and tradition. 
For example, the status of elder, parent, teacher, priest, or leader is recognized because society has accepted these positions over time. 
MacIver and Page say status is a position that gives a person respect, prestige, and influence, apart from personal qualities. 
This means sometimes respect comes from the position itself, not only from the individual person. 
For example, a judge receives respect because of the status of being a judge. A lecturer receives respect because of the position of lecturer. Even before knowing their personal character, society gives some respect to the status. 
Ogburn and Nimkoff give a very simple definition: status represents the position of the individual in the group. 
This is the easiest definition to remember. 
Majumdar says status means the location of the individual within the group. It includes obligations, privileges, rights, and duties. 
This means every status comes with certain benefits and responsibilities. 
For example, a student has the right to learn, ask questions, and receive guidance. But the student also has duties such as attending lectures, submitting assignments, and following university rules. 
Characteristics of Status 
Now let’s understand the characteristics of status one by one. 
Status is determined by the cultural situation of a society. This means status depends on culture. What is respected in one society may not be respected in another society. 


For example, in some societies, older people get very high respect. In some societies, wealth gives high status. In some societies, education gives high status. So status changes according to culture. 
Status is a relative concept. This means status is understood by comparing one person with others. 
For example, a lecturer’s status is understood in relation to students. A manager’s status is understood in relation to employees. A parent’s status is understood in relation to children. Status is not meaningful alone. It becomes meaningful inside relationships. 
Every individual performs roles according to their status. 
For example, because someone has the status of doctor, they are expected to treat patients. Because someone has the status of police officer, they are expected to maintain law and order. Because someone has the status of student, they are expected to study. Every status carries some amount of prestige. Prestige means respect or honour. 
Some statuses carry high prestige, such as doctor, professor, judge, or religious leader. 
Some statuses may carry less prestige depending on society. But every status has some recognized value inside the social system. 
The slide mentions joking and avoidance relationships. This means some societies have special behavior patterns based on status. For example, some relationships allow joking freely, while some require respect and distance. In many cultures, people may joke with cousins or friends but avoid joking too freely with elders, teachers, or in-laws. 
Society is divided into groups based on status. For example, people may be grouped by class, occupation, caste, age, gender, education, or political power. 
Status is also independent of individual temperament or character. This means a person’s status does not always depend on whether they are kind, rude, intelligent, or lazy. Sometimes the position itself gives status. 
For example, a person may be a manager because of their position. Even if their personality is not good, the status of manager still exists. 
Status can be earned or inherited. This leads to the two main types: achieved status and ascribed status. 
Status is also assigned based on group membership and placement. This means society places people in certain positions depending on the groups they belong to, such as family, caste, class, gender, race, age, religion, or occupation. 


Types of Status 
There are two main types of status: 
Ascribed status and achieved status. 
Ascribed Status 
Ascribed status is the status given to a person by birth or by life stage. The person does not choose it. 
It is assigned by society based on things like sex, age, kinship, caste, race, or family background. 
For example, being born male or female is an ascribed status. Being a child of a particular family is an ascribed status. Age status such as child, adult, or elder is also ascribed because it happens naturally in the life cycle. 
In some societies, caste is also an ascribed status because a person is born into it and cannot easily change it. 
So the key idea is: 
Ascribed status is given, not chosen.
Achieved Status 
Achieved status is the status a person gains through effort, talent, education, skill, hard work, and achievement. 
For example, becoming a doctor, lawyer, software engineer, teacher, athlete, lecturer, business owner, or university graduate is achieved status. 
This type of status is not given automatically at birth. A person must work for it. 
So the key idea is: 
Achieved status is earned through effort.
For example, you being a daughter is an ascribed status. But becoming an undergraduate, QA professional, software engineer, or graduate is an achieved status. 
Determinants of Status 
The slide also gives determinants of achieved and ascribed status. 
For achieved status, the determinants include education, occupation, politics, physical strength, and specialization. 


Education gives status because educated people often receive respect and better 
opportunities. Occupation gives status because some jobs are considered more 
prestigious than others. Politics gives status because political power can influence society. Physical strength may give status in some societies or professions, especially in sports or physical labour. Specialization gives status because people with rare skills are valued. 
For ascribed status, the determinants include sex differences, age differences, kinship, caste differences, and racial differences. 
Sex differences mean society may assign different statuses to males and females. Age differences mean children, adults, and elders have different statuses. Kinship means family relationship affects status, such as being a son, daughter, mother, uncle, or grandmother. Caste differences and racial differences are also used in some societies to assign status, although they can create inequality and discrimination. 
Simple Example 
Let’s take one person: a university student. 
She has many statuses at the same time. 
She is a daughter — ascribed status. 
She is a Sri Lankan — ascribed/national status. 
She is an undergraduate — achieved status. 
She may become a software engineer — achieved status. 
She may later become a mother — ascribed by life stage/social relation. 
She may become a team leader — achieved status. 
Each status gives her different respect, duties, rights, and expected behavior. 
Meaning of Role 
A role is the behaviour expected from a person because of their social status. 
For example, if someone has the status of teacher, society expects that person to teach, guide students, prepare lessons, check work, and behave professionally. 
If someone has the status of student, society expects that person to attend lectures, study, submit assignments, respect lecturers, and follow rules. 
So, role is not just any behaviour. It is the behaviour that society expects from a person in a particular position. 
R. Linton says role is the dynamic aspect of status. This is a very important phrase. 


It means status is the position, but role is the active part of that position. 
For example: 
A person is a doctor. That is status. 
A doctor examines patients, gives medicine, and treats illness. That is role. 
A person is a parent. That is status. 
A parent protects, supports, teaches, and cares for children. That is role. 
So role brings status into action. 
Role as Social Action 
The slide says role is the social action or behaviour of the individual in a particular status. 
This means a role shows how a person behaves according to their position in society. 
For example, in a university, a lecturer’s role is different from a student’s role. A lecturer teaches and evaluates. A student learns and completes academic work. Both are in the same institution, but their statuses and roles are different. 
The slide also says role is the way a status is supposed to be filled. 
That means society gives a position, but the person must perform the expected duties of that position. 
For example, simply having the title “team leader” is status. But guiding the team, assigning tasks, solving problems, and taking responsibility are the role. 
Social Activities Are Organized Around Roles 
The slide says all social activities are organized around roles. 
This means society works properly because people perform different roles. 
For example, in a hospital, doctors treat patients, nurses care for patients, pharmacists provide medicine, cleaners maintain hygiene, and administrators manage records. 
Each person has a different role. If people do not perform their roles properly, the whole system can become disorganized. 
The same applies to family, school, university, workplace, government, and religion. 
Role Includes Privileges and Expectations 
A role includes both privileges and expectations. 


Expectations are what society expects you to do. 
Privileges are the benefits or rights connected to your role. 
For example, a lecturer is expected to teach and evaluate students. But the lecturer also has the privilege of authority in the classroom. 
A student is expected to study and follow rules. But the student also has the privilege of learning, asking questions, using university facilities, and receiving guidance. 
So every role includes duties as well as rights. 
Characteristics of Role 
Now let’s understand the characteristics of role one by one. 
1. Action Aspect of Status 
Role is the action aspect of status. 
This means role shows the actions connected to a social position. 
For example, “mother” is a status. Feeding the child, caring for the child, protecting the child, and teaching the child are roles. 
“Police officer” is a status. Maintaining law, protecting people, controlling traffic, and arresting criminals are roles. 
So role is the active behavior expected from a status. 
Also, every role has a cultural basis. This means role expectations depend on culture. 
For example, the role of a daughter, wife, teacher, or elder may be different in Sri Lankan society compared to Western societies. Culture decides what behavior is expected. 
2. Changing Aspect 
Roles change over time. 
What society expected from a person in the past may not be the same today. 
For example, earlier, in many societies, women were expected mainly to stay at home and take care of children. But today, women study, work, lead companies, enter politics, and become professionals. 
Similarly, the role of a teacher has changed. Earlier, teachers mainly used blackboards and books. Today, teachers also use online platforms, slides, videos, and digital tools. 


So roles change according to social values, norms, technology, and time. 
3. Limited Field of Operation 
Roles are context-specific. This means a person performs a role in a particular situation, but not everywhere. 
For example, a manager plays the role of manager in the office. But when that manager goes home, they may play the role of mother, father, husband, wife, daughter, or son. A lecturer has authority in the classroom, but outside university, that same person may be a customer in a shop, a patient in a hospital, or a passenger in a bus. 
So one role does not operate in every place. Roles depend on the social setting. 
4. Not Performed 100% 
It is not always possible to perform a role perfectly. 
Society may expect many things from one role, but in real life, people may not be able to fulfill all expectations completely. 
For example, a student is expected to attend every lecture, study every day, submit all work on time, participate in activities, and maintain good results. But due to stress, health problems, family issues, or workload, the student may not perform everything 100%. 
A parent is expected to provide love, protection, education, discipline, and emotional support. But no parent can be perfect all the time. 
So partial role performance is normal, especially in modern complex society. 
5. Differential Importance 
Not all roles have the same importance. 
Some roles are more important than others depending on the situation. 
For example, for a student, the role of “student” may be more important during exam time. But at home, the role of “daughter” may become more important. 
Some roles are called key roles. These are the most important roles in a person’s life or in a society. 
For example, for a doctor, treating patients is a key role. For a judge, giving fair judgment is a key role. For a teacher, educating students is a key role. 


There are also general roles that many people perform, such as being a citizen, neighbour, friend, or family member. 
6. Inter-connected Characteristics 
Roles require a set of connected qualities, skills, and behaviours. 
For example, a priest is expected to have religious knowledge, ritual knowledge, moral discipline, communication ability, and spiritual leadership. 
A teacher needs subject knowledge, communication skills, patience, discipline, and the ability to guide students. 
A software engineer needs technical knowledge, problem-solving skills, teamwork, and responsibility. 
So a role is not just one action. It includes many connected behaviours and qualities. 
Role Conflict 
Now the slide explains role conflict. 
Role conflict happens when a person faces two or more expectations that clash with each other. 
In simple words, role conflict means: 
Different people or different roles expect different things from you at the same time, and you cannot satisfy all of them fully.
For example, a student may have an exam tomorrow, but family members may expect her to attend an important family function. Here, the student role and family role clash. Role conflict is very common because one person usually has many roles. 
1. Intrasender Conflict 
Intrasender conflict happens when the same person gives contradictory expectations to someone. 
The slide gives the example of parents saying: 
“Be independent,” but at the same time treating the child like a small child. 
Here, the same sender — the parents — gives two opposite messages. 
Another example: 


A lecturer says, “Think creatively,” but also says, “Only write exactly what is in my notes.” This creates confusion because the same person is expecting two opposite things. 
2. Intersender Conflict 
Intersender conflict happens when different people expect different things from the same individual. 
For example, the dean expects a lecturer to focus on academic quality. The faculty may expect administrative work. The student union may expect student-friendly decisions. These different expectations may clash. 
Another simple example: 
A student’s lecturer expects her to submit an assignment. Her parents expect her to help at home. Her friends expect her to come for a group meeting. All expectations come from different people and conflict with each other. 
So intersender conflict means different senders create conflicting expectations. 
3. Inter-role Conflict 
Inter-role conflict happens when one person has multiple roles, and those roles clash. For example, one person may be a student, daughter, friend, employee, and group leader at the same time. Sometimes these roles demand time and energy at the same moment. The slide gives the example of someone who is both a spouse and a deployed soldier. As a spouse, they are expected to support their family. As a soldier, they are expected to serve the country. These two roles can conflict. 
Another example: 
A working mother may face conflict between her role as an employee and her role as a mother. Her workplace expects her to work late, but her child needs care at home. 
This is inter-role conflict 
Norms 
Norms are social rules or guidelines that tell people what behaviour is acceptable and unacceptable in a particular situation. 
In simple words, norms are the “rules of society.” 


These rules may be written or unwritten. Some norms are formal, like laws. Some are informal, like manners, traditions, and customs. 
For example, standing in a queue, saying “thank you,” wearing suitable clothes for a funeral, respecting teachers, not talking loudly in a library, and stopping at a red traffic light are all norms. 
Norms help people understand how to behave in different situations. They also help us predict how others will react to our behaviour. 
For example, when you go to a lecture, you know students are expected to sit, listen, take notes, and not disturb others. Because everyone understands this norm, the lecture can continue smoothly. 
Norms as Social Standards 
The slide says norms are shared standards of desirable behaviour. 
This means norms are accepted by many members of society. They are not only personal habits. They are social expectations. 
For example, in Sri Lankan society, greeting elders respectfully is commonly expected. In a university, submitting assignments on time is expected. In a workplace, being punctual and professional is expected. 
Norms guide relationships between people. They tell us what is polite, rude, respectful, disrespectful, proper, or improper. 
Situational Norms 
Some norms are situational. This means they apply only in certain situations or to certain people. 
For example, the way you behave with friends is different from the way you behave with lecturers. The way you dress for a wedding is different from the way you dress for a funeral. The way you speak in a job interview is different from the way you speak at home. 
So norms change depending on the place, people, culture, and situation. 
Types of Norms 
The slide mentions folkways, mores, and laws. These are three types of norms. 
Folkways are everyday customs and manners. They are not very serious rules, but people expect us to follow them. For example, greeting people, saying “please,” dressing neatly, or 


eating in a socially acceptable way. If we break folkways, people may think we are rude, but the punishment is usually not serious. 
Mores are stronger moral norms. They are connected to society’s ideas of right and wrong. 
For example, honesty, respecting parents, not cheating, not harming others, and being faithful are mores. If someone breaks mores, society reacts strongly. 
Laws are formal written rules created by the government. They are enforced through police, courts, and legal punishment. For example, traffic laws, criminal laws, education laws, and property laws. If someone breaks laws, they may be fined, arrested, or punished legally. So the difference is mainly the level of seriousness and punishment. 
Characteristics of Norms 
Norms are universal across all societies. This means every society has norms. The exact rules may be different, but all societies have rules to guide behaviour. 
Norms are linked to the factual social order. This means norms are connected to the real way society is organized. For example, school norms are connected to the education system, family norms are connected to family life, and workplace norms are connected to employment. 
Norms include value judgements. This means norms are based on ideas of what is good, bad, right, wrong, acceptable, or unacceptable. 
For example, the norm “do not steal” is based on the value that honesty and respect for others’ property are good. 
Norms are relative to situations and groups. This means norms are not exactly the same everywhere. What is normal in one group may be unusual in another group. 
For example, clothing norms may differ between university, temple, office, beach, and party. 
Norms are not always obeyed by all members. This is important. Just because society has norms does not mean everyone follows them. Some people break norms. This is called deviance. 
For example, even though punctuality is expected, some people come late. Even though honesty is valued, some people cheat. 
Norms are usually internalised by people. Internalised means people accept norms inside their mind and follow them naturally, even without punishment. 


For example, many people do not steal not only because they fear law, but because they personally believe stealing is wrong. 
Functions of Norms 
Norms help society survive and protect vulnerable members. For example, norms about caring for children, respecting elders, helping sick people, and protecting disabled people support social survival. 
Norms guide everyday behaviour. They tell people how to act in normal situations such as eating, greeting, studying, working, marrying, travelling, or communicating. 
Norms allow efficient social functioning. If everyone knows expected behaviour, society works smoothly. 
For example, traffic rules help vehicles move safely. Classroom rules help lectures happen properly. Workplace rules help organizations function. 
Norms maintain social order and stability. Without norms, society would be confused and chaotic because people would not know what to expect from each other. 
Norms give cohesion and solidarity to society. This means they help people feel connected because they share common rules and expectations. 
Norms also promote self-control and discipline. People learn to control their behaviour because society teaches them what is acceptable. 
For example, even if someone is angry, norms teach them not to shout, attack, or insult others in public. 
Values 
Now let’s move to values. 
Values are beliefs about what is important, good, bad, desirable, or undesirable in society. Values are deeper than norms. They are the basic ideas behind social rules. 
For example, honesty, respect, freedom, equality, education, family, religion, success, peace, discipline, and kindness can be values. 
If a society values education, then it creates norms like attending school, respecting teachers, studying for exams, and rewarding academic achievement. 
If a society values family, then it creates norms like caring for parents, supporting siblings, and participating in family events. 


So values give meaning and direction to social life. 
Definitions of Values 
M. Haralambos says a value is a belief that something is good and desirable. It defines what is important and worth trying for. 
In simple words, values tell people what goals are important in life. 
For example, if education is valued, people work hard to get degrees. If wealth is valued, people work hard to earn money. If religion is valued, people follow religious practices. H.M. Johnson says values are standards used to compare and judge things as desirable or undesirable. 
This means values help people decide whether something is good or bad. 
For example, using the value of honesty, we judge cheating as bad. Using the value of equality, we judge discrimination as bad. 
Radha Kamal Mukerjee says values are socially approved desires and goals that people learn through conditioning, learning, and socialization. 
This means values are not only personal. Society teaches us what to desire and what to respect. 
For example, from childhood, people may be taught to value education, respect, religion, marriage, career success, or family reputation. 
Characteristics of Values 
Values are abstract in nature. This means values are ideas, not physical things. You cannot touch honesty or respect, but you can see them through people’s behaviour. 
Values give direction and purpose to social life. They guide what people should aim for. Values are often unconsciously upheld. This means people follow values without always thinking about them. For example, a person may automatically show respect to elders because that value has been deeply learned. 
Values are specific to cultural context. Different cultures may value different things. 
For example, some cultures value individual freedom more, while others value family responsibility more. Some societies value tradition strongly, while others value innovation strongly. 


Values are held collectively by a group. This means values are shared by members of society, not only one person. 
Values are often emotionally based. People feel strongly about their values. That is why people may become angry or hurt when someone insults their religion, family, country, or identity. 
There are positive and negative values. Positive values include honesty, kindness, justice, and respect. Negative values may include selfishness, greed, violence, or discrimination, depending on social judgement. 
Values are also subject to change over time. Society’s idea of what is important can change. 
For example, in the past, some societies valued strict gender roles. Today, many societies value gender equality more. Technology has also changed values related to privacy, communication, and education. 
Functions of Values 
Values provide goals or ends. They tell society what people should aim for. 
For example, if success is valued, people aim for achievement. If peace is valued, people avoid violence. If education is valued, people study. 
Values help in conflict resolution. When there is disagreement, values can guide decisions. 
For example, if two people argue, the value of fairness can help solve the issue. 
Values bring stability in interaction. When people share common values, they understand each other better. 
Values provide uniformity in group behaviour. This means people in the same group behave in similar ways because they share similar beliefs. 
Values give legitimacy to rules. This means values make norms meaningful and acceptable. 
For example, the law against stealing is accepted because society values honesty and property rights. 
Values give moral authority to social norms. This means norms become powerful because they are supported by values. 


For example, the norm “respect parents” is powerful because it is supported by the value of family respect. 
Values also help with adjustment between rules. Sometimes norms conflict with each other, and values help decide which rule is more important. 
For example, telling the truth is a norm, but protecting someone from harm is also a norm. 
If telling the truth causes harm, people may use values like compassion and justice to decide what to do. 
Norms vs Values — Easy Difference 
This is the most important part to remember. 
Values are beliefs. Norms are rules.
Value: Respect 
Norm: Stand up when an elder enters, speak politely, do not insult elders. 
Value: Education 
Norm: Attend school, study, submit assignments, respect teachers. 
Value: Honesty 
Norm: Do not lie, do not cheat, do not steal. 
Value: Cleanliness 
Norm: Do not throw garbage on the road. 
So values are the reason behind norms. 
Social Structure Summary 
Now the final slide summarizes the four parts of social structure: status, role, norms, and values. 
Status is the position a person occupies in society. It can be ascribed or achieved. It gives prestige, rights, and responsibilities. 
For example, student, parent, teacher, doctor, citizen, daughter, and leader are statuses. 
Role is the expected behaviour connected to a status. It is the active part of status. 
For example, a student’s role is to study. A teacher’s role is to teach. A parent’s role is to care for children. 
Norms are shared social rules that guide proper behaviour. They include folkways, mores, and laws. They maintain social order. 


For example, queuing, respecting elders, obeying traffic rules, and wearing suitable clothes are norms. 
Values are abstract beliefs about what is good, important, and desirable. They are the foundation of norms. 
For example, honesty, respect, equality, family, education, and justice are values. 
What is Culture? 
Culture means the way of life of a group of people. 
It includes the things people know, believe, value, practice, use, and pass from one generation to another. 
So culture includes things like language, religion, food, clothes, customs, traditions, values, norms, music, art, technology, laws, family patterns, greetings, manners, and beliefs. 
The slide says culture is: 
“A toolkit for the survival of mankind.”
This means culture helps humans survive and live together. Unlike many animals, humans are not born knowing exactly how to live. We learn how to eat, dress, speak, behave, marry, work, worship, respect others, and follow rules through culture. 
For example, in Sri Lankan culture, we learn how to greet elders, how to behave at a temple, how to dress for weddings or funerals, how to speak respectfully, and how to celebrate festivals. These are not biological instincts. They are learned through society. 
Culture is Universal 
The slide says culture is universal. 
This means every human society has culture. There is no human group without culture. 
But all cultures are not the same. Sri Lankan culture, Japanese culture, Indian culture, American culture, Arab culture, and African cultures all have different foods, languages, traditions, religions, and behaviour patterns. 
So the important point is: 
All humans have culture, but cultures differ from society to society.
Culture is one of the main features that separates human society from animal groups. 


Society and Culture are Symbiotic 
The slide says society and culture are symbiotic. This means they depend on each other. A society is a group of people living together. 
Culture is the way those people live. 
Society cannot exist without culture because people need rules, values, language, and customs to live together. 
Culture cannot exist without society because culture needs people to practice it, teach it, and pass it to others. 
For example, Sri Lankan society exists because people share cultural things like language, traditions, religion, family values, education, food habits, and social rules. At the same time, Sri Lankan culture continues because people in society practice it every day. 
Culture is Rule-Based 
Culture gives rules and guidelines for behaviour. 
For a society to survive, people need to know what is allowed and what is not allowed. Culture gives those rules through norms, values, customs, traditions, and laws. 
For example, culture teaches us that stealing is wrong, respecting elders is good, helping others is valuable, and obeying law is necessary. 
Without cultural rules, society would become disorganized because people would not know how to behave. 
Culture is Culturally Relative 
The slide says culture is culturally relative. 
This means the meaning of behaviour depends on the culture. 
The same action can have different meanings in different societies. 
For example, the slide says the “OK” hand sign may have a very different meaning in Tunisia. In one country it may mean “okay,” but in another place it may be offensive or threatening. 
So we should not assume that our cultural meaning is the same everywhere. Context is very important. 
This connects to the idea of cultural relativism, which means understanding a culture according to its own values and meanings, without quickly judging it. 


Importance of Culture 
The slide says humans are not born with all the information needed for survival. We are shaped by nurture, not only by nature. 
This means humans must learn how to live. 
A baby is born with biological needs, but not with culture. The baby does not know language, manners, religion, rules, or customs. These things are learned through socialization. 
Culture teaches us how to satisfy our needs in socially acceptable ways. 
For example, all humans need food. That is biological. But what food we eat, how we cook it, when we eat, whether we use hands, chopsticks, forks, or spoons — these are cultural. All humans need sleep. That is biological. But where people sleep, whether children sleep with parents or separately, and what bedtime routines exist are cultural. 
So culture does not remove biology. It shapes how biological needs are expressed. 
Instinct, Reflex, Drive, and Culture 
The slide gives three biological ideas: instinct, reflex, and drive. 
An instinct is an unlearned biological behaviour common to a species. 
For example, spiders do not learn how to make webs. They naturally know how to do it. A reflex is an automatic response to physical stimuli. 
For example, sneezing, blinking when something comes near your eye, or pulling your hand away from something hot are reflexes. 
A drive is an unlearned impulse that pushes us to satisfy basic needs like food, water, sleep, and reproduction. 
Culture shapes these drives. 
For example, hunger is a biological drive. But culture decides what food is acceptable, how we eat, with whom we eat, and what manners we follow. 
So humans have biological needs, but culture teaches us the “proper” way to handle them. 
Personal Space and Social Distance 
The slide explains personal space, which means the invisible space or “bubble” people keep around themselves. 


There are different levels of distance: 
Intimate distance is very close, usually under 18 inches. This is for close relationships like partners, parents, children, or very close friends. 
Personal distance is around 18 inches to 4 feet. This is common for friends or familiar people. 
Social distance is around 4 to 12 feet. This is used in formal situations like talking to lecturers, officers, strangers, or professionals. 
Public distance is beyond 12 feet. This is used in public speaking, presentations, lectures, or addressing a crowd. 
The important point is that personal space differs depending on relationship and culture. We keep a larger space with strangers and a smaller space with close people. Also, in some cultures, people stand close when talking, while in other cultures, people prefer more distance. 
Context also matters. In a crowded bus or elevator, people stand very close, but that does not mean they are emotionally close. The situation changes the norm. 
Material Culture 
Material culture means the physical things created, used, or valued by a society. 
These are things we can see, touch, taste, or feel. 
Examples include clothes, jewellery, houses, buildings, tools, vehicles, phones, computers, machines, food, art, furniture, books, roads, and technology. 
For example, a sari, temple, clay pot, laptop, bus, phone, wedding jewellery, and traditional food are all material culture. 
Material culture shows how people live and what technology or resources they use. 
Nonmaterial Culture 
Nonmaterial culture means the non-physical parts of culture. 
These are ideas, beliefs, values, norms, language, customs, rituals, gestures, and knowledge. 
For example, respect for elders, religious beliefs, marriage customs, language, politeness, gender expectations, moral values, and funeral rituals are nonmaterial culture. 


You cannot touch “respect” or “honesty,” but you can see them through people’s behaviour. 
So the simple difference is: 
Material culture = physical things 
Nonmaterial culture = ideas and practices 
Example: 
A wedding dress is material culture. 
The belief that marriage is important is nonmaterial culture. 
A temple building is material culture. 
The religious faith and rituals practiced there are nonmaterial culture. 
Perspectives on Other Cultures 
This part explains how people view cultures different from their own. 
Ethnocentrism 
Ethnocentrism means judging other cultures using your own culture as the standard. In simple words, it means thinking, “My culture is normal, and other cultures are strange or wrong.” 
For example, if someone says another culture’s food is disgusting just because it is different from their own food, that is ethnocentrism. 
Ethnocentrism is common, but it can create misunderstanding, prejudice, and disrespect. 
Xenophobia 
Xenophobia means fear, dislike, or hostility toward people from other countries or cultures. 
For example, assuming foreigners are dangerous or bad simply because they are different is xenophobia. 
This can lead to discrimination, racism, violence, or social exclusion. 
Xenocentrism 
Xenocentrism is the opposite of ethnocentrism. 
It means believing another culture is better than your own culture. 


For example, thinking that foreign products, foreign clothes, foreign accents, or foreign lifestyles are always better than local ones is xenocentrism. 
This can make people undervalue their own culture. 
Cultural Relativism 
Cultural relativism means understanding another culture on its own terms, without judging it as superior or inferior. 
This does not mean we must agree with everything. It means we first try to understand why people behave in that way within their culture. 
For example, funeral practices are different around the world. Instead of saying one practice is right and another is wrong, cultural relativism asks: What meaning does this practice have for that society? 
This is very important in sociology because sociologists try to study culture fairly and objectively. 
Values: Standards of Culture 
Values are collective ideas about what is right or wrong, good or bad, desirable or undesirable in a culture. 
Values are the standards people use to judge behaviour. 
For example, a society may value education, family, religion, success, freedom, respect, equality, kindness, discipline, or hard work. 
Values influence norms. If a society values honesty, it creates norms against lying and cheating. If a society values education, it creates norms about schooling, studying, and respecting teachers. 
Robin Williams’ Ten Core US Values 
The slide gives ten core values identified in American culture by Robin M. Williams Jr. 
These include individualism, achievement, work, science and technology, progress, efficiency, equality, morality, freedom, and group superiority. 
You do not need to memorize them deeply unless your lecturer asks, but the main point is this: 
Different societies have different core values. 


For example, American culture often values individual freedom and personal achievement. 
Sri Lankan culture may place more emphasis on family responsibility, respect, religion, education, and community relationships. 
Value Pairs, Clusters, and Conflicts 
Values often exist as pairs. 
For example: 
Good vs evil 
Equality vs inequality 
Freedom vs control 
Honesty vs dishonesty 
Respect vs disrespect 
A value cluster happens when two or more values support each other. 
For example, equality and tolerance support each other because both are connected to fairness. 
A value conflict happens when two values clash. 
For example, freedom and security can sometimes conflict. People may want freedom, but society may introduce rules for safety. Another example is equality and racism. A society cannot fully support both at the same time. 
Ideal Culture and Real Culture 
This is an important exam point. 
Ideal culture means the values and standards a society says it believes in. 
Real culture means how people actually behave in real life. 
For example, a society may say it values equality. That is ideal culture. But if some groups still face discrimination, that is real culture. 
Another example: a university may say it values academic honesty. That is ideal culture. But if some students cheat, that is real culture. 
So ideal culture is what society claims. Real culture is what actually happens. 
Emerging Values 
Cultures change over time, so new values appear. 


The slide gives examples such as leisure, self-fulfilment, physical fitness, youthfulness, and concern for the environment. 
Today, many societies value mental health, work-life balance, sustainability, body fitness, gender equality, and personal happiness more than before. 
This shows that culture is not fixed. It changes with time, technology, economy, education, and global influence. 
Norms: Rules for Behaviour 
Norms are the practical rules of culture. 
If values are beliefs, norms are the behaviour rules based on those beliefs. 
For example: 
Value: Respect 
Norm: Speak politely to elders. 
Value: Cleanliness 
Norm: Do not throw garbage in public places. 
Value: Safety 
Norm: Wear a helmet when riding a motorcycle. 
Norms are conditional, which means they depend on situation and culture. 
Folkways 
Folkways are informal everyday customs. 
They are not very serious rules, but people expect us to follow them. 
Examples include saying “thank you,” standing in a queue, dressing appropriately, table manners, greeting people, and holding the door. 
If someone breaks a folkway, society may think they are rude or strange, but the punishment is usually small. 
Mores 
Mores are stronger moral rules. 
They represent important values of a community. 
For example, do not steal, do not cheat, respect parents, protect children, and be faithful in marriage. 


Breaking mores leads to stronger social disapproval. 
Taboos 
Taboos are acts that are strongly forbidden and almost universally condemned. 
The slide gives incest as an example. Incest means sexual relations between close family members, and it is considered a taboo in almost every culture. 
Taboos create strong emotional reactions such as disgust, shame, or anger. 
Laws 
Laws are formal written norms created by the government and enforced by the state. Examples include traffic laws, criminal laws, marriage laws, property laws, and education laws. 
If someone breaks a law, they can face formal punishment such as fines, imprisonment, or loss of licence. 
So the order from less serious to most serious is usually: 
Folkways → Mores → Taboos/Laws 
Sanctions: Rewards and Punishments 
A sanction is a reward or punishment given for following or breaking norms. 
Sanctions help control behaviour. 
There are four types. 
Positive formal sanctions are official rewards. Examples: salary raise, promotion, academic honours, awards, scholarships. 
Negative formal sanctions are official punishments. Examples: imprisonment, fines, loss of driving licence, expulsion from school. 
Positive informal sanctions are unofficial rewards. Examples: compliments, applause, smiles, approval, being included in a group. 
Negative informal sanctions are unofficial punishments. Examples: gossip, eye rolls, frowns, social exclusion, reputation damage. 
For example, if you get good grades and receive an award, that is a positive formal sanction. 
If your friends praise you, that is positive informal sanction. If someone breaks traffic law 


and pays a fine, that is negative formal sanction. If people gossip about rude behaviour, that is negative informal sanction. 
Cultural Universals 
Cultural universals are cultural practices or institutions found in every human society. They exist because all humans have some common needs. 
Examples include language, funeral rites, music, art, laws, marriage customs, games, religion, myths, toilet training, courtship rituals, and incest taboos. 
The way these things happen may differ, but the basic practice exists in all societies. For example, all societies have some way to deal with death. But funeral practices differ from culture to culture. 
All societies have language, but languages are different. 
All societies have family or partnership systems, but marriage customs differ. 
Types of Culture 
The slide gives several types of culture. 
Ideal Culture 
Ideal culture is what society says it values or wants to achieve. 
Example: “Everyone should be treated equally.” Real Culture 
Real culture is what actually happens. 
Example: People say they support equality, but some groups still experience discrimination. 
Subculture 
A subculture is a smaller cultural group within the larger dominant culture. 
It has its own values, language, style, behaviour, or rituals, but it can still exist inside the larger society. 
Examples include gamers, university students, medical professionals, religious groups, music fan communities, and online communities. 


For example, university students have their own slang, lifestyle, routines, and shared experiences. That is a subculture. 
Counterculture 
A counterculture is a subculture that opposes the dominant culture. 
It does not just differ from mainstream culture; it challenges it. 
Examples include the hippies of the 1960s, punk movements, or groups that reject mainstream lifestyle and values. 
Multiculturalism 
Multiculturalism means accepting and valuing different cultures within one society. It celebrates diversity instead of forcing everyone to become the same. 
For example, a multicultural society respects different languages, religions, food habits, clothing styles, and traditions. 
Assimilation 
Assimilation is the process where a minority group adopts the norms and values of the dominant culture. 
This can happen willingly or because of pressure. 
For example, immigrants may learn the dominant language, change clothing habits, adopt local customs, and follow mainstream social rules to fit into the new society. 
Cultural Etiquette Around the World 
This part shows that manners are different across cultures. 
For example, in some places, being slightly late may be acceptable, while in others it is rude. In some cultures, eating with the left hand is offensive. In some cultures, showing the soles of your shoes is insulting. In some cultures, direct eye contact has a different meaning. 
The main lesson is: 
Do not assume your manners are universal.
When people travel, work internationally, or meet people from different cultures, they need cultural competence. This means understanding and respecting cultural differences. 
Cultural Change 


Culture changes over time. The slide says culture changes through discovery, invention, and diffusion. 
Discovery 
Discovery means finding or understanding something that already exists. 
For example, discovering a medical treatment, natural law, or social pattern. 
The thing already existed, but people gained new knowledge about it. 
Invention 
Invention means creating something new. 
Examples include the wheel, printing press, telephone, computer, internet, mobile phone, and AI systems. 
Inventions can change society deeply. 
For example, smartphones changed communication, education, business, friendship, and entertainment. 
Diffusion 
Diffusion means the spread of culture from one group or society to another. 
For example, fast food, K-pop, Hollywood movies, jeans, English language, social media trends, and Western fashion spread across many countries through diffusion. 
Cultural Levelling 
Cultural levelling happens when cultures become more similar because of diffusion. 
For example, because of globalization, many people around the world use smartphones, wear similar clothes, watch the same movies, eat similar fast food, and use similar social media platforms. 
This can connect people, but it can also reduce cultural uniqueness. 
Culture Lag 
Culture lag is a concept by William Ogburn. 
It means material culture changes faster than nonmaterial culture. 
In simple words, technology changes quickly, but laws, values, ethics, and social rules take time to catch up. 


Example: Computers and the internet developed quickly, but privacy laws came later. 
Another example: AI technology is growing fast, but society is still trying to create proper rules about ethics, privacy, jobs, and education. 
Culture Shock 
Culture shock is the confusion, anxiety, or discomfort people feel when they enter a very different culture. 
For example, a Sri Lankan student going to another country may feel culture shock because food, language, weather, manners, teaching style, and social rules are different. 
At first, the person may feel lonely or confused. Over time, they adjust. 
Cultural Imperialism 
Cultural imperialism means one powerful culture spreads strongly into other societies and influences or dominates them. 
For example, American movies, fast food, fashion, pop music, and brands have spread widely across the world. 
This can influence local culture, sometimes even replacing local traditions. 
High Culture and Popular Culture 
High culture refers to cultural activities usually associated with upper or educated classes. 
Examples include classical music, opera, ballet, theatre, art galleries, and literary fiction. Popular culture refers to culture enjoyed by large numbers of ordinary people. 
Examples include sports, TV dramas, sitcoms, social media, pop music, movies, fashion trends, memes, and concerts. 
Popular culture spreads quickly, especially through mass media and the internet. 
Global Village 
The term global village was popularized by Marshall McLuhan. 
It means technology has made the world feel smaller and more connected. 
Through the internet, phones, TV, social media, and instant communication, people can connect across countries immediately. 


For example, a trend starting in Korea can become popular in Sri Lanka within days. A news event in America can be seen worldwide instantly. 
This creates rapid cultural diffusion. But the slide also asks an important question: does technology truly bring people closer, or does it only create the feeling of connection? 
Sociological Perspectives on Culture 
Different sociological theories explain culture in different ways. 
Functionalist Perspective 
Functionalists see culture as something that helps society survive and remain stable. 
Culture gives rules, values, roles, and meanings. It helps people cooperate and meet needs. 
For example, family culture helps raise children. Religious culture gives moral guidance. Education culture teaches knowledge and discipline. 
So functionalists believe culture supports social order. 
Conflict Theory 
Conflict theorists say culture can be used by powerful groups to control others. 
The ruling class can spread ideas that make inequality seem normal. 
For example, if society teaches poor people that poverty is only their personal fault, they may not question unfair economic systems. 
So conflict theory sees culture as a tool of power and control. 
Symbolic Interactionism 
Symbolic interactionists study culture at the micro level. 
They focus on how people create and use symbols, meanings, language, gestures, and everyday interaction. 
For example, a handshake, a smile, a wedding ring, a national flag, or a religious symbol all carry meanings. 
People create culture through daily interaction, but later that culture also shapes people. 
Postmodern Perspective 


Postmodernists argue that much of modern culture is based on simulation or constructed reality. 
Television, social media, and the internet show edited, filtered, and curated versions of reality. 
For example, Instagram lifestyles may look perfect, but they are often carefully selected and edited. People may start accepting these images as real life. 
So postmodern theory questions what is real and what is constructed in modern culture. 
Cultural Diversity Blunders 
The slide gives examples of companies making mistakes because they did not understand culture or language properly. 
For example, a product name or advertisement can mean something very different in another language. 
The lesson is: 
Culture and language matter in communication, business, marketing, and global relations.
If people ignore cultural differences, they may insult others or fail in business. 
Key Takeaways 
The main points from this whole section are: 
Culture is universal because every human society has culture. 
Culture is learned, not biologically inherited. We learn it through socialization, imitation, family, education, media, religion, and everyday life. 
No culture should be judged as naturally superior. Sociologists try to understand cultures fairly using cultural relativism. 
Culture constantly changes through discovery, invention, and diffusion. 
Language shapes thought because the words and grammar we use influence how we understand the world. This idea is connected to the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis. 
Norms form a hierarchy of expectations: folkways, mores, taboos, and laws.

Deviance & Crime 
Sociology of Deviance, Social Control & Criminal Justice 
Key Definitions 
Deviance means the violation of social norms. Social norms are the expected rules and behaviours accepted by society. Everyone can be deviant at some time or in some places because norms change depending on society, culture, and situation. 
Stigma means the disapproval attached to disobeying expected norms. It is like a mark of social disgrace that separates the deviant person from the rest of society. 
Crime is a form of deviance where formal penalties are imposed by society. A crime is any act prohibited by law and punishable by the government. 
What is Deviance? 
Deviance is not fixed. It is ever-changing, meaning what is considered deviant can change over time. A behaviour that was seen as deviant in the past may be accepted today, and something accepted in one society may be deviant in another. 
Deviance is also culturally and historically located. This means deviance depends on culture and time period. It always exists in relation to what society considers “normal.” Deviance is socially constructed. Even though some behaviours may be seen as completely immoral, society ultimately decides what is deviant and what is not. 
Deviance is also context dependent. The same behaviour may be ignored in one situation but labelled deviant in another. Social status, place, situation, and the people involved can influence whether behaviour is considered deviant. 
Nature of Deviance 
Society determines what is deviant and also decides the consequences for deviant behaviour. What is considered deviant can vary from one society to another and from one time period to another. 


A person does not usually become labelled as deviant because of one single act. Often, deviance is linked to repeated behaviour, serious acts with negative consequences, or being detected and stigmatized by society. 
For example, serious acts such as rape, murder, and robbery are strongly labelled as deviant because they harm others and violate both social norms and laws. 
Status and Deviance 
Social status affects how deviance is judged. People with low social status, such as the poor or ethnic minorities, are more likely to be perceived as deviant. 
People with higher social status, such as doctors, priests, or respected professionals, may be less likely to be labelled as deviant, even when they engage in questionable behaviour. 
Both actions and individuals can be defined as deviant. Sometimes, even whole groups can be labelled as deviant by society. 
Social Functions of Deviance 
According to Émile Durkheim 
Émile Durkheim explained that deviance can have important social functions. 
Deviance unifies the group because it creates a boundary between people who conform and those seen as outsiders. When society rejects deviant behaviour, people may feel more connected through shared values. 
Deviance clarifies norms because it helps people understand what behaviours are acceptable and unacceptable. When someone breaks a norm, society responds and reminds others of the expected behaviour. 
Deviance diffuses tension because some deviant acts allow people to release stress or frustration without completely disturbing society. 
Deviance promotes social change because it can show problems in society that need to be fixed. Sometimes, people who challenge norms help society move forward. 
Deviance provides jobs because crime and deviance create roles for judges, lawyers, police officers, prison workers, criminologists, and crime reporters. 


Deviance opens new paths because challenging social norms can lead to new ideas, creativity, and innovation. 
Types of Deviance 
There are two main types of deviance: positive deviance and negative deviance. 
Positive Deviance 
Positive deviance means behaviour that over-conforms to social expectations. This happens when a person follows a norm too strongly or excessively. 
For example, society expects people to be fit and healthy. But extreme bodybuilding and steroid use can be considered positive deviance because the person is over-conforming to fitness and body expectations. 
Other examples include anorexia, which over-conforms to body ideals, extreme academic or work perfectionism, and religious zealotry beyond social norms. 
Negative Deviance 
Negative deviance means behaviour that under-conforms to social expectations. This happens when a person fails to follow accepted social norms. 
Examples include obesity, which may be seen as under-conforming to fitness norms, criminal behaviour and law-breaking, social withdrawal and isolation, and refusal to follow social etiquette. 
Theories of Deviance, Crime & Justice 
Study Notes 
1. Theories of Deviance 
Theories of deviance explain why people break social norms and why some behaviours are labelled as deviant. These explanations can be studied at three levels: 
Micro level focuses on individuals and small-group interactions. 
Meso level focuses on groups, communities, and institutions. 
Macro level focuses on large social structures such as inequality, capitalism, patriarchy, and power. 


Structural-Strain Theory 
Robert K. Merton 
Merton’s Structural-Strain Theory explains deviance as a result of the gap between 
cultural goals and legitimate means. 
In American society, people are encouraged to achieve goals such as success, wealth, 
and material achievement. However, not everyone has equal access to education, jobs, 
money, or opportunities to reach those goals legally. 
Because of this pressure, some people experience strain. This can lead to anomie. 
Anomie 
Anomie means a state of normlessness, confusion, or unclear rules. It happens when 
society’s norms are no longer clear or do not guide people’s behaviour properly. 
Merton’s Modes of Adaptation

Simple Explanation 
Conformity means accepting society’s goals and using legal ways to achieve them. 
Innovation means accepting society’s goals but using illegal or unacceptable ways to 
achieve them. 
Ritualism means rejecting big success goals but still following rules and routines. 
Retreatism means rejecting both society’s goals and its accepted methods. 
Rebellion means rejecting existing goals and methods and replacing them with new ones. 


2. Micro-Level Theories of Deviance 
Social Control Theory 
Travis Hirschi 
Social Control Theory explains that people conform because they have strong bonds with society. When these bonds are weak, people are more likely to become deviant. 
Hirschi identified four main social bonds: 
Social Bond Meaning 
Attachment Strong connection to family, friends, teachers, or groups 
Commitment Investment in future goals such as education or career 
Involvement Participation in approved activities 
Belief 	Belief in social rules, values, and laws 
Simple Example 
A student who is close to family, committed to studies, involved in university activities, and believes in rules is less likely to engage in deviant behaviour. 
Rational Choice Theory 
Rational Choice Theory says people decide whether to commit deviance by weighing the costs and benefits. 
A person may ask: 
“Will I gain something from this?” 
“Will I get caught?” 
“What punishment will I receive?” 
If the benefit seems greater than the punishment, the person may choose deviant behaviour.

Edwin Sutherland 


Differential Association Theory explains that deviance is learned through interaction with others. 
People learn deviant behaviour from the people they spend time with, such as friends, family, peer groups, or gangs.

Simple Example 
If a teenager spends more time with friends who steal or use drugs, they may learn and accept those behaviours as normal. 
Labeling Theory 
Howard Becker 
Labeling Theory says behaviour is not naturally deviant. Instead, behaviour becomes deviant when society labels it as deviant. 
According to Becker, deviance is created by social reaction. A person becomes deviant when others define and treat them as deviant. 
Primary Deviance 
Primary deviance refers to a deviant act that is not strongly labelled. The person does not build their identity around it. 
Example: A student cheats once but is not caught or labelled as a cheater. 
Secondary Deviance 
Secondary deviance happens when a person accepts the deviant label and begins to live according to it. 


Example: A student labelled as a troublemaker may start acting like one because society already expects that behaviour. 
Labeling Theory — Deep Explanation 
Being labelled as deviant can reinforce deviant behaviour. This can create a self-fulfilling prophecy, where society’s expectations shape the person’s future behaviour. 
People with less power in society are more likely to be labelled as deviant.

3. Macro-Level Theories 
Conflict Theory 
Quinney / Marx 
Conflict Theory explains deviance as a result of social inequality and power differences. According to this view, powerful groups decide what is deviant and what is criminal. Laws often protect the interests of the ruling class. 
Main Ideas 
Elites define deviance in ways that benefit themselves. 
The ruling class may label threatening behaviour as deviant. 
Law enforcement is often directed more toward crimes committed by lower classes. Poor and powerless people are more likely to be arrested and punished. 


Conflict is unavoidable in capitalist societies because power and wealth are unequally distributed. 
Simple Example 
Street crime may receive more attention and punishment than corporate crimes, even though white-collar crimes may cause greater financial and social damage. 
Feminist Theory 
Feminist Theory explains crime and deviance by focusing on gender inequality and patriarchy. 
It argues that women’s experiences of crime are shaped by male-dominated social structures. 
Main Ideas 
Abuses suffered by women are rooted in patriarchal and capitalist systems. 
Gendered division of labour affects women’s roles and opportunities. 
Children are socialized into gender roles from a young age. 
Cultural attitudes toward crimes against women differ depending on women’s status in society. 
Women are less often in positions where certain crimes can be committed. 
Women’s crimes may differ from men’s because of gendered social conditions. 
4. Shaming Theory 
John Braithwaite 
Shaming Theory explains how society’s reaction to wrongdoing can either increase or reduce deviance. 
There are two types of shaming: 
Disintegrative Shaming 
Disintegrative shaming rejects and stigmatizes the wrongdoer. The person is pushed out of society and treated as permanently bad. 


This can lead to more deviance because the person becomes isolated and may identify more with deviant groups. 
Reintegrative Shaming 
Reintegrative shaming condemns the wrong act but forgives and supports the wrongdoer. The person feels guilt but is given a chance to return to society. This discourages further deviance. 
Simple Difference 
Disintegrative shaming says: 
“You are a bad person.” 
Reintegrative shaming says: 
“What you did was wrong, but you can repair it and return to society.”

6. Crime 
Definition of Crime 


Crime is any act that is labelled as criminal by those in authority, prohibited by law, and punishable by the government. 
Crime is a type of deviance, but not all deviance is crime. 
7. Types of Crime 
Violent / Street Crime 
Violent crime includes crimes such as murder, robbery, and assault. 
Robbery means taking property through violence or threat. Violent crimes are serious but represent a smaller percentage of all crimes. 
Property Crime 
Property crime involves stealing or damaging another person’s property. 
Examples include:

White-collar crime is often less publicized than street crime, but it can be more costly and harmful.

9. Ways of Measuring Crime 
Crime can be measured in different ways. No single method is perfect, so using several methods together gives a better understanding. 
Uniform Crime Reports — UCR 
The UCR is published annually by the FBI. It records crimes reported to the police. 
Limitations 
Not all crimes are reported. 
Not all complaints reach official statistics. 
Crimes involving family or friends may be hidden. 
Police decisions may be influenced by the attitude of the complainant or suspect. 


Self-Reported Surveys 
In self-reported surveys, people anonymously report their own criminal or deviant behaviour. 
This method is useful because it captures crimes that may never be reported to the police. It is especially useful for studying victimless crimes and underreported offences. 
Victimization Surveys 
Victimization surveys ask people whether they have been victims of crime, whether they reported it or not. 
This method gives a fuller picture of crime than police records alone. 
Triangulation 
Triangulation means using all three methods together: UCR 
Self-reported surveys 
Victimization surveys 
This gives the most accurate picture of crime.

The criminal justice system includes: 
Police

Police 
Police have the most immediate control over who gets arrested. 


Police discretion means police officers have power to decide how to handle situations. Factors that may affect arrest decisions include: 
Seriousness of the offence

Racial profiling is an important issue in policing. 
Courts 
Courts decide guilt or innocence through trials. 
However, many cases are settled through plea bargaining. 
Plea Bargaining 
Plea bargaining is when the accused agrees to plead guilty to a lesser charge in order to reduce punishment or speed up the legal process. 
It also helps reduce court caseloads. 
Corrections 
Corrections include punishments and supervision such as: Prison 
Parole

Juvenile justice deals with offenders under 18. 
Juvenile offenders are given legal rights similar to adults, but the system often focuses more on rehabilitation than punishment.

Four Functions of Prison 
Prisons are expected to perform four main functions. 
Retribution means punishing offenders for the harm they caused. 
Deterrence means discouraging the offender and others from committing crimes. Rehabilitation means reforming offenders so they can return to society. 
Social protection means preventing offenders from committing more crimes while they are imprisoned. 
Recidivism 
Recidivism means reoffending after being released from prison.

12. Alternatives to Prison 
Social Capital 
Social capital means rebuilding social networks, shared values, trust, and cooperation within communities. 
It helps offenders reconnect with positive support systems. 
Shock Probation 
Shock probation means releasing a first-time offender early after they experience prison life. 
The aim is to discourage them from reoffending. 
Day Treatment and Halfway Houses 
These are community-based programmes that provide supervision and support as offenders transition from prison back into society. 
Restitution 
Restitution means the offender gives money or service to the victim or community to repair the harm caused. 
13. Restorative Justice 
What is Restorative Justice? 
Restorative Justice is a process where victims, offenders, and the community come together to deal with the effects of crime and decide how to repair the harm. Restorative justice focuses on healing instead of only punishment. 
Traditional Justice Asks 
Who committed the crime? 
What laws were broken? 
How will we punish the offender? 


Restorative Justice Asks 
What harm was done? 
What needs to be done to repair the harm? 
Who is responsible for repairing the harm? 
14. Van Ness Principles of Restorative Justice 
Principle 1 — Healing 
Justice should work to heal victims, communities, and offenders harmed by crime. 
Principle 2 — Involvement 
Victims, offenders, and communities should actively participate in the justice process as early and fully as possible. 
Principle 3 — Roles 
Government and community have different roles. Government should preserve just order, while the community should establish just peace. 
15. Values of Restorative Justice 
Restorative Justice is based on several important values: 
Crime is an act against a person or community, not only against the state. Crime harms victims, communities, and offenders. 
Crime creates an obligation to make things right. 
Victims, offenders, and communities should participate in the response. The victim’s perspective is central. 
Offender accountability means accepting responsibility and repairing harm. The community must ensure fairness and protection. 
Restoration should replace punishment as the main goal. 
Restitution should be common. 
Success should be measured by repair done, not by severity of punishment. 


Crime control is also a community responsibility.

17. Key Takeaways 
Deviance is a violation of changing and socially constructed norms. It can help society by clarifying norms, uniting people, and promoting social change. 
Theories of deviance explain deviance from different perspectives. Strain Theory focuses on blocked opportunities, Labeling Theory focuses on social labels, Control Theory focuses on social bonds, Differential Association explains learning deviance, Conflict Theory focuses on power, and Feminist Theory focuses on gender inequality. 
Crime is deviance that is formally prohibited by law and punishable by the government. Types of crime include violent crime, property crime, white-collar crime, organized crime, hate crime, and state crime. 
The criminal justice system includes police, courts, corrections, and juvenile justice. 
However, high recidivism raises questions about whether punishment alone is effective. 
Restorative justice focuses on repairing harm, involving victims and offenders, and rebuilding relationships rather than only punishing offenders.

Part I — The Case 
PixelPath Technologies (Pvt) Ltd 
PixelPath Technologies (Pvt) Ltd is a fictional Sri Lankan tech startup founded in 2018 and located in Colombo 03. 
The company has around 180 employees, mostly IT graduates. It builds ERP systems for industries such as: 
Garment industry 
Hospitality industry 
Retail industry 
PixelPath also has a Singapore partnership with DataBridge Asia. 
One of its major clients is MegaMart Lanka, and the system handled data belonging to 12,000 employees. 
Main Characters in the Case 
Kasun Wijesinghe — Falsely Accused 
Kasun is a Junior Software Developer. 
He is a state university graduate from Kurunegala and earns around LKR 85,000 per month. 
Although he discovers suspicious activity, he is falsely accused of wrongdoing. 
Nirosha Fernando — Actual Perpetrator 
Nirosha is a Senior Data Engineer. 
She comes from a Colombo private school background, has a foreign degree, and earns around LKR 240,000 per month. 
She has a close relationship with the CEO, which gives her protection and influence inside the company. 


Amara Dissanayake — Whistleblower 
Amara is an IT Support Technician. 
Amara is a non-binary LGBTQ+ employee and earns around LKR 60,000 per month. Amara supports Kasun and speaks up, but later faces exclusion and harassment in the workplace. 
The Incident — What Happened? 
In March 2024, PixelPath was contracted to build MegaMart Lanka’s payroll system. This system handled sensitive data belonging to 12,000 employees, including: 
NIC details 
Bank details 
Biometric data 
During the development phase, Nirosha secretly gave herself persistent admin access to the live production database. This was a serious violation because she accessed sensitive information without proper authorisation. 
Six months later, Kasun discovered unusual database queries coming from an external IP address. He traced the data movement to a third-party server connected to a data 
brokerage outside Sri Lanka. 
Before Kasun could formally report the issue, Nirosha accused him first. She told the CEO that Kasun was “snooping” in restricted systems. 
As a result, Kasun was suspended without pay. The company did not report the incident to CERT|CC for more than three weeks. PixelPath also issued a false press statement suggesting that no customer data had been exposed. 
The Aftermath 
Impact on Kasun 
Kasun was suspended for four months without pay. 
He was professionally stigmatised because the company’s press statement made him appear guilty before the investigation was complete. 
Recruiters later saw his name in news aggregators, which damaged his job opportunities. 


Although an independent forensic consultant finally cleared him, his career, reputation, and finances were seriously harmed. 
Impact on Nirosha 
Nirosha was protected because of her higher status, salary, foreign degree, and connection with the CEO. 
A junior HR officer raised concerns about Nirosha, but those concerns were ignored. Instead of facing criminal charges, Nirosha resigned voluntarily and received a six-month severance package. 
No charges were filed against her. 
Impact on Amara 
Amara was excluded from team meetings after supporting Kasun. 
Amara’s access privileges were downgraded without explanation. 
Amara also received derogatory WhatsApp messages about their gender identity. 
After three months, Amara resigned because of the toxic work environment. 
Part II — Deviance, Norms & Stigma 
Deviance is ever-changing, socially constructed, culturally located, and context dependent. This means that society and institutions decide what is deviant and who gets labelled as deviant. 
In this case, the same organisation treated different people differently based on status, power, and identity. 
Nirosha’s Act 
Nirosha committed a clear technical and legal norm violation. 
She gave herself unauthorised admin access and exfiltrated sensitive data. 


This is formally deviant and criminal because it violates professional ethics, data security rules, and cybercrime law. 
However, she was not immediately labelled deviant because she had high organisational status. 
Kasun’s Act 
Kasun’s behaviour was ethically motivated but procedurally irregular. 
He investigated suspicious database activity and tried to understand what was happening.

Page 1 — Sociological Theory: Study Pack Overview 
This first page introduces the topic Sociological Theory. A sociological theory is like a lens or framework that helps us understand society. Society is very complex, so sociologists use theories to explain questions like: why do people behave in certain ways, why does inequality exist, how does society stay stable, and how does society change? 
The contents page shows four main areas. First is Functionalism, which explains society as a stable system. Second is Conflict Theory, which explains society through power, inequality, and struggle. Third is Symbolic Interactionism, which explains society through daily interaction and shared meanings. Fourth is Max Weber, who connects several perspectives and explains society using ideas such as class, status, authority, 
bureaucracy, and religion. 
The main idea of this page is: no single theory explains everything, so we study different theories to understand society from different angles. 
Page 2 — What Is Sociological Theory? 
This page defines sociological theory as a set of ideas that helps us understand how society works, why society works that way, how society changes, and who benefits from social arrangements. 
There are three types of theory shown here. Macro theories look at large-scale society. They study big structures like government, education, economy, religion, class, and law. Functionalism and Conflict Theory are macro theories because they look at society as a whole. 
Micro theory looks at small-scale daily interaction. It studies how people create meaning through communication, symbols, gestures, and everyday behaviour. Symbolic 
Interactionism is a micro theory. 
Multi-paradigm theory combines different perspectives. Max Weber is placed here because his ideas connect power, class, meaning, religion, and bureaucracy. He cannot be placed under only one theory. 


The second part compares the four perspectives. Functionalism asks, “How does society hold together?” Conflict Theory asks, “Who has power and who is oppressed?”Symbolic Interactionism asks, “How do people create shared meaning?” Weber asks, “How do ideas and power shape society?”
Page 3 — Functionalism Introduction 
This page introduces Functionalism. Functionalism is a macro perspective, which means it studies society as a whole. It sees society as a stable system made up of different parts that work together. 
A simple example is the human body. The heart, lungs, brain, and stomach have different functions, but together they keep the body alive. Functionalists see society in a similar way. 
Family, education, religion, economy, law, and government all have functions that help society survive and remain stable. 
Functionalism mainly studies social structures and institutions, solidarity and social order, manifest and latent functions, and social change and equilibrium. 
So, the key idea is: every part of society exists because it performs a function for society.
Page 4 — What Is Functionalism? 
This page explains Functionalism more clearly. Functionalism sees society as a system of interconnected parts working together to maintain stability and order. 
A social structure is an organized part of society, like family, school, religion, politics, or economy. These structures are important because they help society function. 
Norms and values are also important in Functionalism. A society needs shared values and rules so that people know how to behave. For example, if people value education, they follow school rules and respect teachers. If people value law and order, they obey laws. 
The page also mentions stability. Functionalists believe institutions contribute to society by performing specific functions. For example, schools educate children, families socialize children, and religion gives moral guidance. 
The page also introduces key functionalist theorists: Auguste Comte, Herbert Spencer, Émile Durkheim, Talcott Parsons, and Robert Merton. 


Page 5 — Auguste Comte and Herbert Spencer 
This page explains two early functionalist thinkers. 
Auguste Comte is often called the founder of sociology. He believed sociology should study society scientifically. He introduced two important ideas: social statics and social dynamics. 
Social statics means the stable parts of society that hold society together, such as family, religion, law, and institutions. Social dynamics means social change, or how society develops over time. So Comte studied both stability and change. 
Herbert Spencer compared society to a living organism. He believed society evolves just like biological organisms. He was influenced by Darwin’s ideas. Spencer used the idea of survival of the fittest, meaning stronger societies survive and weaker ones may decline. 
However, his idea of Social Darwinism is criticised because it can justify inequality by saying poor or weak groups “naturally” fail. This is dangerous because it ignores unfair social systems and structural problems. 
Page 6 — Émile Durkheim and Four Types of Suicide 
This page explains Émile Durkheim, one of the most important sociologists. Durkheim believed society is held together by solidarity. 
He explained two types of solidarity. Mechanical solidarity exists in traditional societies where people are similar to each other. They share the same religion, values, lifestyle, and work. Example: small villages. 
Organic solidarity exists in modern industrial societies where people are different but depend on each other. For example, doctors, teachers, farmers, engineers, drivers, and business owners do different jobs, but society needs all of them. This interdependence creates social unity. 
The next part explains Durkheim’s famous study of suicide. He argued that suicide is not only a personal psychological issue. It is also influenced by social integration and social control. 
He identified four types: egoistic, altruistic, fatalistic, and anomic suicide. 


Egoistic suicide happens when a person is socially isolated. Altruistic suicide happens when a person is too strongly absorbed into a group and sacrifices themselves. Fatalistic suicide happens under extreme control. Anomic suicide happens when social rules break down, often during economic crisis or rapid change. 
The important lesson is: even personal actions can have social causes. 
Page 7 — Talcott Parsons and Robert Merton 
This page explains Talcott Parsons and Robert Merton. 
Parsons saw society like a bicycle wheel. In a bicycle wheel, many spokes are connected to the centre. If one spoke breaks, the wheel may still work, but if many spokes break, the whole wheel becomes unstable. In the same way, society has many institutions connected together. If one institution fails, it can affect the whole society. 
Parsons believed social systems tend to stay stable unless a strong force changes them. This idea is called social inertia. It means society does not change easily; it usually continues in the same direction. 
Robert Merton developed middle-range theory. Instead of creating one huge theory for all society, he studied smaller parts of society carefully. 
His famous ideas are manifest functions and latent functions. Manifest functions are intended and obvious results. 
Latent functions are unintended and hidden results. 
For example, the manifest function of school is to educate students. But the latent function may be making friends, building networks, learning discipline, or finding future partners. 
Page 8 — Manifest and Latent Motives + Criticisms of Functionalism 
This page gives a practical example of Merton’s idea using the decision to buy a Cadillac. 
A person may say they buy a Cadillac because it is comfortable or high quality. These are manifest motives — the clear and accepted reasons. 
But there may also be latent motives, such as showing wealth, gaining status, or impressing others. These are hidden motives that the person may not openly admit. 
The second part gives criticisms of Functionalism. 


One criticism is that Functionalism may ignore power and inequality. It focuses too much on stability and may forget that some people benefit more than others. 
Another criticism is that it can support the status quo, meaning it may defend the existing system even when the system is unfair. 
It may also overlook minorities, underestimate inequality, and fail to explain rapid social change. It can also be too abstract and difficult to test in real life. 
So Functionalism is useful for understanding stability, but weak in explaining conflict and inequality. 
Page 9 — Functionalism Key Takeaways + Conflict Theory Introduction The top section summarises Functionalism. 
Comte introduced social statics and social dynamics. Spencer compared society to an organism. Durkheim explained solidarity and suicide. Parsons explained society as a system seeking stability. Merton introduced manifest and latent functions. The criticisms remind us that Functionalism may ignore power and inequality. 
The second part introduces Conflict Theory. 
Conflict Theory is also a macro theory, but it sees society differently. Functionalism sees society as stable and cooperative. Conflict Theory sees society as a place of struggle, inequality, and competition. 
The main idea is that society has limited resources, and groups compete for wealth, power, status, and opportunities. Those with power try to maintain control, while disadvantaged groups struggle for change. 
Page 10 — What Is Conflict Theory? 
This page explains Conflict Theory in detail. 
Conflict Theory sees society as an ongoing struggle over scarce resources. Resources include money, land, education, jobs, political power, legal protection, and social status. The page shows the idea of a power pyramid. This means society is unequal. A small group at the top has more wealth and power, while many people at the bottom have less. 


Conflict theorists believe conflict is built into the system because not everyone has equal access to resources. 
This theory applies not only to class, but also to race, gender, marriage, religion, criminal justice, environment, and international relations. 
The second part shows how conflict theorists see society. They argue that economic wealth and power shape institutions. They also say discrimination is structural, meaning inequality is built into social systems, not just caused by individual attitudes. 
Conflict theory also highlights class consciousness. This means workers must understand their shared position before they can challenge exploitation. 
Page 11 — Five Conflict Theorists and Karl Marx 
This page introduces five conflict theorists: Karl Marx, Harriet Martineau, W.E.B. Du Bois, Jane Addams, and John Bellamy Foster. 
Then it focuses on Karl Marx. Marx believed that the history of society is the history of class struggle. 
He divided capitalist society mainly into two classes: bourgeoisie and proletariat. The bourgeoisie are the capitalist class. They own factories, businesses, land, machines, and other means of production. 
The proletariat are the working class. They do not own production resources, so they sell their labour to survive. 
Marx argued that capitalism creates inequality because the capitalist class profits from workers’ labour. 
The page also explains false consciousness. This means workers may fail to understand that they are being exploited. They may accept the system as normal because dominant ideas make inequality appear natural. 
Page 12 — Harriet Martineau and W.E.B. Du Bois This page explains two important conflict theorists. 
Harriet Martineau is considered one of the first female sociologists. She studied gender, slavery, and social inequality. She showed that society in America did not give equal 


opportunities to all people. She argued that inequality was not accidental; it was structured into society. 
Her key idea is that economic inequality and gender inequality are connected. She showed that we cannot understand women’s lives without looking at social power and economic systems. 
W.E.B. Du Bois studied race and racism. He argued that the major problem of the twentieth century was the “colour line,” meaning racial division. 
He studied the African American experience and showed that poverty among Black Americans was not due to personal failure, but due to slavery, discrimination, and structural racism. 
He also introduced the idea of double consciousness. This means oppressed people may see themselves through their own identity and also through the eyes of the dominant society. 
Page 13 — Jane Addams and John Bellamy Foster 
This page explains Jane Addams and John Bellamy Foster. 
Jane Addams was connected to the settlement house movement. She helped establish Hull House in Chicago. Settlement houses supported poor and immigrant communities by providing education, services, and social support. 
Her work showed that poverty is not simply a personal failure. It is connected to social conditions, lack of support, inequality, and lack of opportunity. 
John Bellamy Foster is a modern conflict theorist who focuses on capitalism and ecology. 
He argues that capitalism damages both workers and the environment because markets prioritise profit over human and ecological needs. 
He criticises the idea that markets can solve every problem. According to him, capitalism often creates problems that it has no incentive to fix, especially environmental damage. 
Page 14 — Criticisms of Conflict Theory + Voting Rights Example This page gives criticisms of Conflict Theory. 
One criticism is that it can be too radical. It may focus too much on conflict and revolution and not enough on cooperation. 


Another criticism is that it can oversimplify society by seeing everything mainly as conflict between powerful and powerless groups. 
Some critics say conflict theory does not explain why competition can sometimes improve society. Others say it ignores consensus, shared values, and social unity. 
The second part gives the example of Susan B. Anthony, who was arrested for voting unlawfully in 1872. At that time, women in the United States did not have the legal right to vote. Her action challenged the political system. 
This example shows conflict theory in action. People in power made rules that excluded women, and activists challenged those rules. Social change happened through struggle. 
Page 15 — Conflict Theory Key Takeaways + Symbolic Interactionism Introduction The top section summarises Conflict Theory. 
Marx focused on class conflict. Martineau showed that gender and slavery were linked to structural inequality. Du Bois explained racism and double consciousness. Jane Addams showed that poverty is structural and worked for social reform. Foster connected capitalism with environmental destruction.

Symbolic Interactionism is a micro perspective. It does not mainly focus on large institutions. Instead, it studies everyday interaction, communication, symbols, meanings, identity, and interpretation. 
The key idea is that society is built through meaning. People do not simply react to the world; they interpret it. 
Page 16 — What Is Symbolic Interactionism? 
This page explains Symbolic Interactionism clearly. 
Symbolic Interactionism focuses on how communication influences the way people interact and create social reality. 
A symbol is something that carries meaning. Words, gestures, objects, signs, clothes, flags, wedding rings, emojis, and body language can all be symbols. 


For example, a wedding ring is not just metal. It symbolises marriage and commitment. A national flag is not just cloth. It symbolises identity, country, and belonging. 
Symbolic Interactionism says meaning is not automatic. People create meaning through interaction. 
It is a micro theory because it studies individuals and small interactions, but it can still help us understand larger society. 
The page also says individuals have power to co-create their world. That means people shape society through everyday communication, but society also shapes them. 
Page 17 — Core Principles and George Herbert Mead 
This page explains core principles of Symbolic Interactionism and introduces George Herbert Mead. 
One principle is that symbols are arbitrary. This means a symbol does not naturally have meaning; people give it meaning. 
For example, the word “tree” means tree in English, but other languages use different sounds. The meaning is created socially. 
Another principle is that shared definitions create order. If people agree on meanings, interaction becomes smooth. If meanings differ, conflict or misunderstanding can occur. The page also says identity is fluid and ongoing. This means the self is not fixed forever. We develop our identity through interaction with others. 
George Herbert Mead explained the relationship between symbols, mind, self, and society. 
He believed we develop the self by taking the role of others. We learn to see ourselves from other people’s perspectives. 
In simple terms, we understand who we are because we interact with others. 
Page 18 — Herbert Blumer and Erving Goffman 
This page explains two symbolic interactionist thinkers. 
Herbert Blumer created the term “Symbolic Interactionism.” He gave three principles. 


First, people act toward things based on the meanings those things have for them. Second, meaning comes from social interaction. Third, meaning is changed through interpretation. For example, a lecturer’s feedback may mean encouragement to one student, but criticism to another student. The meaning depends on interpretation. 
Erving Goffman developed the idea of dramaturgy. He compared social life to theatre. 
According to Goffman, people are like performers. We present ourselves differently depending on the situation. 
Front stage is where we perform for others, like in class, interviews, presentations, or social media. 
Back stage is where we relax and stop performing, like at home or with very close friends. He also explained impression management, which means controlling how others see us. 
Page 19 — Howard Becker and Labelling Theory 
This page explains Howard Becker and his labelling theory. 
Labelling theory says deviance is not only about what a person does. It is also about how society reacts and labels that person. 
For example, if a student breaks a small rule and society labels them as “bad” or “troublemaker,” that label can affect how others treat them and how the student sees themselves. 
Becker argued that labels have power. Powerful groups can define what is normal and what is deviant. 
The page also explains self-fulfilling prophecy. This means if someone is repeatedly labelled in a certain way, they may start acting according to that label. 
For example, if a child is constantly called lazy, they may start believing they are lazy and stop trying. 
The second part gives criticisms of Symbolic Interactionism. It may ignore larger social structures like poverty, law, economy, and power. It may focus too much on individual interaction and not enough on big social systems. 
Page 20 — Symbolic Interactionism Key Takeaways + Max Weber Introduction 


This page summarises Symbolic Interactionism. 
It explains that society is built through shared meanings. Mead explained mind, self, and society. Blumer explained that meaning comes from interaction and interpretation. Goffman explained life as performance. Becker explained labelling and deviance. 
Then the page introduces Max Weber. 
Weber is presented as a multi-paradigm theorist because his work connects Conflict Theory, Functionalism, and Symbolic Interactionism. 
He studied class, status, party, authority, bureaucracy, religion, capitalism, and interpretive understanding. 
The important thing about Weber is that he believed society cannot be explained only through money or class. Ideas, values, religion, status, and authority also shape society. 
Page 21 — Who Was Max Weber? 
This page explains Max Weber as a special sociologist who cannot be placed under only one theory. 
Weber wrote partly as a response to Karl Marx. Marx focused strongly on class and economics. Weber agreed that class is important, but he argued that inequality is more complex. 
Weber believed society is shaped by many dimensions: economic class, social status, political power, religion, values, and ideas. 
He also believed values shape society. Ideas are not just reflections of the economy. They can influence behaviour and even economic systems. 
For example, religious beliefs may influence how people work, save money, spend money, and understand success. 
So Weber’s main contribution is that he made sociology more multidimensional. 
Page 22 — Weber as Conflict Theorist and Functionalist 
This page shows how Weber connects to both Conflict Theory and Functionalism. 
As a Conflict Theorist, Weber believed inequality is multidimensional. He accepted Marx’s idea that class matters, but said class alone cannot explain everything. 


He explained three dimensions of stratification: class, status, and party. 
Class is economic position. It depends on wealth, income, job, and market opportunities. Status is social honour or prestige. Some people may have high respect even if they are not the richest. 
Party is political power. It refers to groups that influence decisions, such as political parties and interest groups. 
As a Functionalist, Weber studied bureaucracy. He believed bureaucracy is a rational form of organization. It helps large organizations work efficiently using rules, hierarchy, 
specialization, and clear responsibilities. 
Examples include government offices, universities, hospitals, and corporations. 
Page 23 — Weber’s Types of Authority and Symbolic Interactionism This page explains Weber’s three types of authority. 
Traditional authority is based on customs, traditions, and long-established practices. Example: kings, chiefs, elders, hereditary rulers. 
Charismatic authority is based on the personal qualities of a leader. People follow the leader because they believe the leader is extraordinary. Example: revolutionary leaders, religious prophets, inspirational political leaders. 
Legal-rational authority is based on laws, rules, and formal positions. Example: modern governments, courts, police, universities, and corporations. 
The page also explains Weber as connected to Symbolic Interactionism. 
Weber believed values and beliefs influence social action. He introduced the idea of Verstehen, which means interpretive understanding. In simple words, sociologists should understand the meaning behind people’s actions from their point of view. 
For example, to understand religious behaviour, we should not only observe rituals. We should understand what those rituals mean to the people practicing them. 
Page 24 — Protestant Ethic and Value-Free Sociology 
This page explains one of Weber’s most famous works: The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism. 


Weber argued that certain Protestant religious beliefs helped support the growth of capitalism. 
The idea was that hard work, discipline, saving money, and reinvesting profit became religiously meaningful. Over time, these values supported capitalist economic behaviour. So Weber showed that religion and ideas can shape economic systems. This challenged Marx’s view that economy is always the main base of society. 
The second part explains value-free sociology. 
Weber believed sociologists should study society objectively. This means they should try not to let personal bias control their research. 
However, he also admitted that values can influence what topics researchers choose to study. So complete neutrality is difficult, but sociologists should still aim for fairness and evidence-based analysis. 
Page 25 — Weber Across the Three Paradigms 
This page summarises how Weber fits into three perspectives. 
In Conflict Theory, Weber explains inequality through class, status, and party. He shows that power is not only economic. 
In Functionalism, Weber explains bureaucracy as a system that helps modern organizations work efficiently. Bureaucracy supports order and predictability. 
In Symbolic Interactionism, Weber explains that values and meanings shape behaviour. His idea of Verstehen focuses on understanding people’s subjective meanings. 
The key takeaways include Weber as a multi-paradigm thinker, the three dimensions of inequality, bureaucracy, Protestant ethic, value-free sociology, and Verstehen. 
The main point: Weber bridges different theories and gives a more complete way to study society.
Page 26 — Four Perspectives at a Glance 
This page compares all four perspectives. 
Functionalism focuses on social stability. It asks how society holds together. It studies structures, functions, norms, and social order. 


Conflict Theory focuses on power and inequality. It asks who benefits and who is oppressed. It studies class, race, gender, wealth, and struggle. 
Symbolic Interactionism focuses on meanings and daily interaction. It asks how people create shared reality. It studies symbols, language, identity, labelling, and performance. 
Max Weber connects all three. He studies class, status, party, authority, bureaucracy, religion, capitalism, values, and interpretation. 
The second table compares how the theories answer key questions. 
For example, what holds society together? Functionalists say shared values and 
institutions. Conflict theorists say domination and unequal power. Symbolic interactionists say shared meanings. Weber says legitimate authority and rational organization. 
So this page is very useful for exam revision because it shows the differences clearly. 
Page 27 — No Single Theory Tells the Whole Story + Deviance & Crime Intro This page gives the final message: 
No single theory tells the whole story.
This means society is too complex to be explained by only one theory. Functionalism helps us understand stability. Conflict Theory helps us understand power and inequality. Symbolic Interactionism helps us understand meaning and daily interaction. Weber helps connect these perspectives. 
A good sociologist does not depend on only one theory. They compare theories and use the best ideas from each.

1. What are Ethics and Morality? 
In normal conversation, people often use ethics and morality as the same thing. But in philosophy, there is a small difference. 
Morality means the beliefs, values, and rules people follow about what is right and wrong in life. It is about how people actually live and behave. 
For example, “Do not lie,” “Respect others,” and “Help people in need” are moral ideas. Ethics means the formal study of morality. It asks whether our moral beliefs are correct, reasonable, fair, or justified. Ethics is also called moral philosophy. 
Simply: 
Word 	Simple Meaning 
Morality What people believe is right or wrong 
Ethics 	The study and analysis of those beliefs 
Example: 
Morality says, “Stealing is wrong.” 
Ethics asks, “Why is stealing wrong? Are there any situations where it can be justified?” 
2. Four Branches of Ethics 
Ethics has four main branches. 
1. Descriptive Ethics 
Descriptive ethics studies what people actually believe about morality. 
It does not say whether something is right or wrong. It only describes moral beliefs in a society. 
Example: 
A researcher studies how Sri Lankan people think about arranged marriage, LGBTQ+ rights, or workplace honesty. 
Main question: 
What do people believe?


2. Normative Ethics 
Normative ethics asks what people should do. 
It tries to create theories about right and wrong actions. 
Example: 
Should we tell the truth even if it hurts someone? 
Should companies protect user privacy even if it reduces profit? 
Main question: 
What should people do?
Important theories under normative ethics include: Utilitarianism 
Deontology 
Virtue Ethics 
Rights Ethics 
3. Applied Ethics 
Applied ethics uses moral theories to solve real-life problems. 
Example areas: 
Business ethics 
Medical ethics 
Environmental ethics 
IT ethics 
AI ethics 
Professional ethics 
Example question: 
Is it ethical for a company to collect user data without clear consent? 
Main question: 
How do we apply ethics to real situations?
4. Meta-Ethics 


Meta-ethics studies the meaning of moral words and ideas. 
It asks deeper questions like: 
What does “good” mean? 
Are moral statements true or false? 
Is morality universal or based on culture? 
Example: 
When we say “lying is wrong,” is that a fact, an opinion, or a cultural belief? 
Main question: 
What do moral terms really mean?

4. How to Identify Moral Issues 
Moral issues are situations where we must decide what is right, wrong, fair, unfair, harmful, or responsible. 
Ethics helps us think carefully instead of judging quickly. 
There are two main areas: 


Evaluating Others’ Behaviour 
Sometimes people use ethics to judge others. But we must be careful because we may not know their full situation or intentions. 
Important questions: 
Do we truly know why they acted that way? 
Do we have the right to judge them? 
Should we intervene? 
Are we being hypocritical? 
Example: 
If a student copies an assignment, we may say it is wrong. But ethics asks us to think deeper: Why did they do it? Pressure? Fear? Lack of support? Still, does that make it acceptable? 
Searching for Meaning in Our Own Life 
Ethics is also about deciding what kind of life we want to live. 
It asks: 
What is truly good for me? 
What is valuable? 
What kind of person should I become? 
What responsibilities do I have? 
So ethics is not only about judging others. It is also about improving our own life and decisions. 
5. Basic Moral Orientations 
This part asks: 
On what basis do we make moral decisions? Different moral theories give different answers. 
6. Main Moral Theories 


1. Divine Command Theory 
This theory says morality comes from God. 
Main idea: 
Do what God commands.
Example: 
If a religion teaches that helping the poor is good, followers see it as a moral duty. 
Strength: Gives clear moral guidance. 
Weakness: People follow different religions, so they may disagree about God’s commands. 
2. Utilitarianism 
Utilitarianism says the right action is the one that produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number of people. 
Main idea: 
Make the world better.
This is a consequentialist theory, because it judges actions by their consequences. 
Example: 
If a hospital has one available medicine and five patients need it, utilitarianism asks which decision will save or benefit the most people. 
Strength: Practical and outcome-focused. 
Weakness: It may ignore the rights of a minority if the majority benefits. 
3. Virtue Ethics 
Virtue ethics focuses on character. 
Main idea: 
Be a good person.
It does not only ask, “What should I do?” 
It asks, “What kind of person should I be?” 
Important virtues include: 


Wisdom 
Courage 
Temperance 
Justice 
Honesty 
Kindness 
Example: 
A good IT professional is not only skilled but also honest, responsible, respectful, and fair. Strength: Focuses on personal character. 
Weakness: It may not always give clear rules for difficult situations. 
4. Ethics of Duty / Deontology 
Deontology says some actions are right or wrong regardless of consequences. 
Main idea: 
Do your duty.
Immanuel Kant is the main philosopher here. 
Example: 
Lying is wrong because honesty is a moral duty, even if lying might bring some benefit. Strength: Protects important moral principles. 
Weakness: Sometimes strict rules may create difficult situations. 
5. Rights Ethics 
Rights ethics says all people have basic rights that must be respected. 
Main idea: 
Respect rights.
Examples of rights: 
Right to life 
Right to privacy 
Right to liberty 
Right to property 
Right to fair treatment 


In IT, this is very important because users have rights over their personal data. 
Example: 
A company should not misuse customer data because users have a right to privacy. 
6. Ethical Egoism 
Ethical egoism says each person’s main moral responsibility is to look after themselves. 
Main idea: 
Watch out for yourself.
Example: 
A person chooses the job that benefits them most, even if others are disappointed. Strength: Encourages self-responsibility. 
Weakness: Can become selfish and ignore others’ suffering. 
7. Social Contract Theory 
This theory says morality comes from agreements between people in society. 
Main idea: 
Honour agreements.
Example: 
People agree to follow laws because laws help society stay safe and organised. Strength: Explains why rules are needed in society. 
Weakness: Some people may be excluded from fair agreements. 
8. Evolutionary Ethics 
Evolutionary ethics says moral behaviour developed because it helped humans survive. 
Main idea: 
Survive and thrive.
Example: 
Cooperation, caring for children, and helping group members helped early humans survive. 


Strength: Connects morality with human survival. 
Weakness: Survival does not always mean something is morally good. 
7. Utilitarianism in Detail 
Utilitarianism focuses on consequences. 
An action is good if it increases happiness and reduces suffering. 
Important thinkers: 
Epicurus said pleasure and reducing suffering are important for a good life. 
Jeremy Bentham introduced the idea of calculating pleasure and pain. 
John Stuart Mill said actions are right when they promote general happiness. 
Simple example: 
If a government builds a hospital instead of a luxury building, utilitarianism supports the hospital because it benefits more people. 
8. Virtue Ethics in Detail 
Virtue ethics is one of the oldest moral theories. 
Plato identified four cardinal virtues: 
Wisdom 
Courage 
Temperance 
Justice 
Aristotle explained that morality is developed through practice and habit. 
Example: 
A person becomes honest by repeatedly practising honesty. 
Key idea: 
A good action comes from a good character. 
For virtue ethics, the main question is: 
What kind of person should I be?


9. Kant’s Moral Theory 
Kant believed morality should be based on reason, not emotions, tradition, or consequences. 
His main idea is called the Categorical Imperative. 
Simple meaning: 
Before doing something, ask yourself: 
Can everyone do this same action and society still work properly?
Example: 
If I lie whenever it benefits me, can everyone lie whenever it benefits them? 
No. Trust would break down. Therefore, lying is wrong. 
Kant also said we must treat people as ends, not merely as means. 
That means we should not use people only for our benefit. 
Example in IT: 
Using users’ personal data without consent treats them as tools for profit, not as people with dignity. 
10. Ethical Relativism, Absolutism and Pluralism 
This section asks: 
Are moral truths universal, or do they depend on culture?
Ethical Relativism 
Ethical relativism says moral standards differ from culture to culture. 
What is considered right in one society may be considered wrong in another. 
Example: 
Different cultures may have different views about marriage, gender roles, food habits, punishment, or family duties. 
Main idea: 
There is no single universal moral standard. 


Strength: Encourages tolerance and understanding. 
Weakness: It becomes difficult to criticize harmful cultural practices. 
Ethical Absolutism 
Ethical absolutism says some moral truths are always true, everywhere. 
Example: 
Torturing innocent people is always wrong. 
Murder is always wrong. 
Slavery is always wrong. 
Strength: Protects universal human rights. 
Weakness: It may ignore cultural context. 
Ethical Pluralism 
Ethical pluralism accepts that cultures differ, but it also believes some basic values may be shared. 
Example: 
Cultures may differ in customs, but most societies value fairness, safety, honesty, and care. 
This is a balanced view between relativism and absolutism.

This combines rule-based and result-based thinking.

24. Moral Development — Kohlberg 
Kohlberg explained how people’s moral thinking develops. 
Level 1: Pre-Conventional 
People behave to avoid punishment or gain reward. 
Example: 
“I won’t copy because I might get caught.” Level 2: Conventional 
People behave to gain approval or follow rules. 


Example: 
“I won’t copy because my lecturer and classmates will think badly of me.” Level 3: Post-Conventional 
People behave based on principles and universal values. 
Example: 
“I won’t copy because honesty and fairness are important, even if no one catches me.” Most people stay around the conventional level. Moral professionals should try to reach post-conventional thinking.

26. Moral Dilemmas 
A moral dilemma happens when two moral duties conflict. 
Example: 
You promised your company confidentiality, but you discover the company is harming customers. 
Should you stay loyal to the company? 
Or should you protect the public? 
Both sides have moral reasons. 


27. How to Resolve a Moral Dilemma 
Use these six steps: 
	1.Identify the moral factors. 
2.Gather facts. 
3.Rank the moral issues. 
4.Consider alternatives. 
5.Talk to trusted colleagues. 
6.Make a balanced decision. 
Simple idea: 
Do not decide emotionally or quickly. Think carefully and gather evidence.

Moral Management in Sri Lanka’s Export-Oriented Garment Industry 
This case study is about ethical management inside a Sri Lankan garment factory. The company is called Meridian Apparel (Pvt) Ltd, located in the Katunayake Export Processing Zone. 
The main idea of the slide is: 
Managerial decision-making cannot be ethically neutral.
That means every management decision affects people in some way. A manager cannot say, “This is only a business decision, not an ethical issue.” In real workplaces, decisions about working hours, safety, salary, leave, production targets, and welfare all have ethical effects.

In this industry, managers face pressure to:

But at the same time, they must also protect: 
workers’ health, 
rest time,

So the ethical question is: 
Can a company increase productivity without harming workers?

3. Moral Management 
Ms. Dilrukshi Perera — Head of HR 
Ms. Dilrukshi is an example of a moral manager. 
A moral manager does not only follow the law. They go beyond the law and actively protect people. 
She introduced a confidential multilingual grievance channel before buyers required it. 
This means workers could complain safely in a language they understood. This is very important because many garment workers may fear speaking openly due to job insecurity or power differences. 
She also started wellbeing programmes for hostel workers. Hostel workers are often away from home, so they may face loneliness, stress, health issues, and limited family support. 
Most importantly, she refused to approve 12-hour shifts. 
This shows ethical leadership because she considered workers’ health, rest, and dignity, not only production targets. 
Simple meaning 
Ms. Dilrukshi thinks: 


“Workers are human beings, not just production units.” That is moral management. 
4. Amoral Management — Unintentional 
Mr. Suresh Karunaratne — Production Manager 
Mr. Karunaratne is not shown as a bad person. He is shown as ethically blind. 
This means he made a business decision without thinking about its human impact. He redesigned shift rosters to increase output. From a business side, the decision looked successful. 
The factory achieved: 
19% improvement in output per worker-day, USD 1.4 million order delivered on time, 
daily shift increased from 8 hours to 10 hours, Saturday overtime added. 
So, from a production view, he may think: 
“This is a good decision. Output increased. Buyer order was delivered. Workers got overtime pay.” 
But he failed to think about the workers’ real lives. 
5. The Human Cost of the Roster Change 
The slide shows that the business result looked good, but the human impact was serious. Workers had to work longer hours. Saturday overtime was concentrated on the day many workers usually used to travel home. 
Many garment workers in EPZ areas are migrant workers. They may come from far places and stay in hostels or boarding houses. Saturday may be their only chance to visit family, children, parents, or sick relatives. 
Because of the new roster: 
Saturday absenteeism increased to 34%, compared to the normal facility average of 8%. 


This means many workers could not manage the new schedule. 
Workers were treated for stress-related symptoms within six weeks. 
There were 14 formal grievances, the highest monthly total in Meridian’s history. One trained machinist resigned because she could not visit her sick child for three weeks. This shows that the decision damaged worker wellbeing.

They should treat worker wellbeing as a production responsibility, not only an HR issue

# Lecture 08 – Sustainable Development: Detailed Notes

## 1. Introduction to Sustainable Development

### Definition

Sustainable Development refers to:

> "Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."

It aims to balance four key dimensions:

* **People** – Human well-being and social justice
* **Planet** – Environmental protection and conservation
* **Prosperity** – Economic growth and development
* **Technology** – Innovation that supports sustainability

### Core Principles

* Intergenerational equity
* Environmental stewardship
* Social inclusion
* Economic viability
* Responsible technological advancement

# Study Note: Professional Practice

## 1. What is a Profession?

* **Core Definition:** A profession is officially defined as a calling that demands specialized knowledge and typically involves a long, intensive period of academic preparation.
* **The Social Covenant:** It holds a distinct position within society, existing as neither a pure trade nor a pure academic discipline. It is explicitly characterized by a formal covenant with society to deploy its specialized expertise responsibly and in the interest of the public.

### Its Role and Meaning in Society

* **Public Trust Guarantee:** The privilege of self-regulatory power is granted to professions by society. In return, practitioners promise to place the interests of their clients and the wider public above personal financial gain. This fundamental bargain serves as the exact foundation of professional status.
* **Knowledge Custodianship:** Professions operate as designated custodians for complex and esoteric bodies of knowledge that a lay person cannot accurately evaluate. This baseline information asymmetry is what fully justifies both their social privileges and professional duties.
* **Social Stratification Role:** From a sociological perspective, professions hold an intermediate class position. They provide a pathway for social mobility through credentialed expertise and ultimately shape societal inequality, civic life, and access to services.
* **Civic and Democratic Function:** Independent professions, particularly law and medicine, function as vital counterweights to state and market power. The operational autonomy they possess protects civil liberties in circumstances where state actors overreach.

---

## 2. Defining Work Categories: Job, Vocation, Occupation, and Profession

The framework establishes clear boundaries and key features to differentiate between common types of work:

### Job

* **Definition:** Any paid activity that involves exchanging labor strictly for wages.
* **Key Features:** Payment stands as the sole purpose of the activity. The work is transactional, entirely defined by the employer, lacks specific entry requirements, and renders the worker easily substitutable.
* **Examples:** Retail cashier, delivery driver, and data entry clerk.

### Vocation

* **Definition:** A calling or work pursued primarily for its intrinsic meaning or spiritual fulfillment, separate from the rate of pay.
* **Key Features:** The core meaning of the work transcends any material remuneration. It is driven by a deep calling rather than financial compensation, carries high personal commitment, features a distinct service orientation, and may or may not be regulated.
* **Examples:** Priesthood/ministry, social work, and idealized teaching.

### Occupation

* **Definition:** A broad category of work defined fundamentally by its activities. Notably, all professions are classified as occupations, but not all occupations qualify as professions.
* **Key Features:** This is an activity-defined category that may require specific skills, but it implies absolutely no self-regulation. It functions primarily as a statistical or census classification, making it a "category without a covenant".
* **Examples:** Unregistered engineer, IT developer, and uncertified accountant.

### Profession

* **Definition:** A highly specialized occupational group that claims exclusive expertise, strictly enforces ethical standards, and serves the public interest under a binding covenant with society.
* **Key Features:** It is defined by an exclusive body of knowledge, mandatory entry standards, a self-regulation framework equipped with disciplinary sanctions, and an explicit fiduciary duty to the public.
* **Examples:** Solicitor/barrister, medical doctor, and registered architect.

---

## 3. The 6 Core Characteristics of a Profession

A professional designation is structurally maintained by six distinct pillars:

1. **Systematic Body of Knowledge:** Practicing a profession requires total mastery over a coherent, specialized, and theoretically grounded body of knowledge. This knowledge is completely inaccessible to laypersons without extended training and is explicitly codified in textbooks, journals, and educational curricula.
2. **Formal Education and Training:** Entry into the field demands prolonged, formal academic preparation, which typically means earning a university degree alongside completing supervised practical training. This rigorous credentialing process ensures baseline minimum competence and acts as a strict gatekeeper to professional status.
3. **Autonomy and Self-Regulation:** Professionals hold a high degree of occupational autonomy. The state formally delegates regulatory power directly to the profession, granting it the legal right to determine the operational standards of its own work and to discipline or sanction members who fall below those lines.
4. **Ethical Code and Fiduciary Duty:** Practitioners are bound by explicit codes of ethics that mandate putting client and public interest far above personal gain. Enforceable fiduciary duties—specifically including loyalty, confidentiality, care, and full disclosure—form the moral core of professional status.
5. **Service Orientation and Altruism:** The ultimate defining orientation of any profession is service directed toward clients, the public, and society at large. This structural altruism separates a profession from a pure trade, ensuring that a practitioner’s primary commitment is the welfare of those served rather than maximizing profit.
6. **Professional Community and Culture:** Members share a highly distinctive occupational identity, culture, and community. This identity is deeply socialized throughout training, continually maintained by recognized professional bodies, and actively expressed through a shared language, core values, and strict norms of practice.

---

## 4. The Liberal Profession

* **Definition:** A liberal profession is one that explicitly requires advanced intellectual and academic education. It must be practiced personally under one's own responsibility, meaning it is not subordinate to an employer's direction. It directly serves the public interest and is governed by a legally recognized professional body that wields the power of self-regulation, including the specific ability to confer or withdraw a practitioner's right to practice.
* **Historical Origins:**
* *Ancient Roots:* Tied historically to the original liberal arts (*artes liberales*), which represented the education of free citizens and distinguished "learned practice" from "servile trade".
* *Medieval Universities:* Formalized by early universities into the three original "learned professions": Theology, Law, and Medicine, with each requiring specialized university training.
* *19th-Century Professionalization:* Marked by the creation of modern statutory registration, mandatory entry examinations, and disciplinary machinery via organizations like the Law Society (1825), British Medical Association (BMA, 1832), and Royal Institute of British Architects (RIBA, 1834).
* *European Union Legal Definition:* Officially codified under Directive 2005/36/EC, which defines liberal professions as requiring specific qualifications and operating under protected professional titles.


* **Defining Hallmarks:** Hallmarks include advanced intellectual and academic preparation, personalized practice that cannot be delegated, absolute personal responsibility for advice and outcomes, and a legally recognized, protected professional title. It also demands a self-regulatory body with the power to admit and expel, an explicit public interest obligation, an enforceable code of conduct, and strict confidentiality obligations to clients.
* **Recognized Examples:** The designated fields include Law, Medicine, Pharmacy, Architecture, Accountancy, Engineering, Surveying, and Dentistry.

---

## 5. Professional Frameworks: Codes of Practice vs. Codes of Conduct

Professional governing bodies manage compliance and ethics through two distinct behavioral instruments:

* **Code of Practice:** This specifies exactly **how** professional tasks must be performed. It is operationally focused, providing technical standards, procedural requirements, and safe working methods. These are frequently sector-specific and can carry statutory force (such as the CDM or Construction Design and Management Regulations).
* **Code of Conduct:** This specifies exactly **how** professionals must behave. It focuses heavily on principles, integrity, and professional accountability, dictating ethical obligations, core values, and the proper relationships maintained with clients, colleagues, and the public.

### Universal Principles Found in Professional Codes

Across varying disciplines, six universal principles form the bedrock of professional behavior:

1. **Integrity:** Adhering strictly to moral and ethical principles.
2. **Competence:** Maintaining the advanced skills and contemporary knowledge required to practice safely and effectively.
3. **Confidentiality:** Protecting client information and honoring public trust.
4. **Independence:** Remaining entirely free from conflicting interests or toxic external pressures.
5. **Public Interest:** Directing specialized skills toward the safety, health, and betterment of wider society.
6. **Accountability:** Accepting full personal responsibility for actions, decisions, and practice outcomes.

* *19th-Century Professionalization:* Marked by the creation of modern statutory registration, mandatory entry examinations, and disciplinary machinery via organizations like the Law Society (1825), British Medical Association (BMA, 1832), and Royal Institute of British Architects (RIBA, 1834).
* *European Union Legal Definition:* Officially codified under Directive 2005/36/EC, which defines liberal professions as requiring specific qualifications and operating under protected professional titles.


* **Defining Hallmarks:** Hallmarks include advanced intellectual and academic preparation, personalized practice that cannot be delegated, absolute personal responsibility for advice and outcomes, and a legally recognized, protected professional title. It also demands a self-regulatory body with the power to admit and expel, an explicit public interest obligation, an enforceable code of conduct, and strict confidentiality obligations to clients.
* **Recognized Examples:** The designated fields include Law, Medicine, Pharmacy, Architecture, Accountancy, Engineering, Surveying, and Dentistry.

## 5. Professional Frameworks: Codes of Practice vs. Codes of Conduct

Professional governing bodies manage compliance and ethics through two distinct behavioral instruments:

* **Code of Practice:** This specifies exactly **how** professional tasks must be performed. It is operationally focused, providing technical standards, procedural requirements, and safe working methods. These are frequently sector-specific and can carry statutory force (such as the CDM or Construction Design and Management Regulations).
* **Code of Conduct:** This specifies exactly **how** professionals must behave. It focuses heavily on principles, integrity, and professional accountability, dictating ethical obligations, core values, and the proper relationships maintained with clients, colleagues, and the public.

### Universal Principles Found in Professional Codes

Across varying disciplines, six universal principles form the bedrock of professional behavior:

1. **Integrity:** Adhering strictly to moral and ethical principles.
2. **Competence:** Maintaining the advanced skills and contemporary knowledge required to practice safely and effectively.
3. **Confidentiality:** Protecting client information and honoring public trust.
4. **Independence:** Remaining entirely free from conflicting interests or toxic external pressures.
5. **Public Interest:** Directing specialized skills toward the safety, health, and betterment of wider society.
6. **Accountability:** Accepting full personal responsibility for actions, decisions, and practice outcomes.
